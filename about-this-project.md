# ResumeStitch AI — Project Overview

## What this is

A personal, self-hosted job application assistant. It takes a job description and one of my parsed master resumes, tailors a resume and cover letter against it, checks the result for ATS fit and factual accuracy, and lets me review and approve everything before anything is saved. It's built with a MERN stack (MongoDB, Express, React, Node) and an agentic core written with LangChain.js and LangGraph.js.

For the detailed technical spec — every node, the data model, and the diagrams — see the companion document, *ResumeStitch AI — Development Workflow*.

## Why I'm building it

I'm re-entering the job market after roughly two years away, targeting medium-to-large UK companies whose stack matches mine — Auto Trader, Checkout.com, Peak AI, Faculty AI, Luminance, Monzo, and Revolut among them. Tailoring a resume well for each application takes real time, and doing it by hand for dozens of applications doesn't scale.

This project solves that directly, and it does double duty: it's also a legitimate AI-engineering portfolio piece in its own right, since it demonstrates the exact skills I'm positioning around — RAG-style retrieval, agentic pipelines with LangGraph, and disciplined guardrail design — applied to a real problem I actually have, not a toy demo.

## What matters most about how it works

Three principles shaped almost every design decision in the workflow document:

- **Never invent anything about me.** Tailoring is retrieval and rephrasing from my own resume bullets, never open generation. A dedicated verification step checks every tailored claim against its source before it's ever shown to me. The goal isn't just "technically true" — it's a resume I can actually defend if someone asks about it in an interview.
- **I approve everything before it's used.** No resume or cover letter gets saved or exported without me reviewing the diff against my original wording first.
- **Don't optimize toward the wrong number.** The pipeline avoids reshaping my resume just to inflate an ATS score — retries are driven by specific, named problems (a missing requirement, an unsupported claim, poor readability), not a single score threshold that's easy to chase and easy to game.

## Who this is for

Just me, for now. It's scoped around one person managing a handful of master resumes and working through job descriptions one at a time — not a multi-tenant product, not built for scale. That scope is intentional: it kept the architecture simple enough to actually finish.

## Current state

**Phase 1 is complete.** The full pipeline described in the workflow document is built and working end-to-end: resume upload/parsing, the tailoring loop, deterministic verification, ATS scoring, human approval (with re-check and suggest-missing-skills), retry policy, resume deletion cascade, and on-demand `.docx`/`.xlsx` export. Real applications have been run through it against actual job postings (e.g. ConnexAI, Found Talent), which surfaced and led to fixing real bugs beyond the pipeline logic itself:

- Four pipeline-logic bugs: a stale skill-gap list after a retry, hand-edits getting silently overwritten by a later retry, a bullet added to plug a JD skill gap not reliably surviving the model's own selection, and bullet selection being able to drop an entire employer from the tailored resume.
- Two resume-parsing bugs, found by uploading a real resume of my own: a summary paragraph silently dropped entirely on a resume with no explicit `SUMMARY` heading, and education entries getting their degree/institution fields scrambled on a common layout the parser hadn't accounted for.
- A tailored-summary quality problem: the AI-generated summary kept restating the same achievement numbers already shown in the bullets directly below it. Took three prompt iterations to actually fix — the first two (a bare "include one metric" rule, then a reasoning-based "don't repeat a bullet's number" rule) both still failed a live test; the fix was realizing metrics in this system can *only* ever come from a bullet, so the rule had to be a flat "no metric at all, only years-of-experience" rather than a conditional one.

See `key-decisions-log.md` for the reasoning behind each fix.

Since then, two further features landed: the resume-upload form now deterministically pre-fills itself (name, title, contact details, a suggested label) from the file the moment it's selected, still fully editable before saving; and the Skills list is now editable in two independent scopes (permanently on the master resume, or scoped to just one application's export), with each skill chip color-coded to show whether it's genuinely backed by real bullet text — both purely display/export conveniences that never touch scoring or gap analysis. The loading and empty states across the app were also reworked with real spinners, an animated hourglass loader, and proper icon+message empty states, replacing plain "Loading…" text throughout.

Testing the pipeline against resumes very different from my own (a security/hospitality CV, a Flutter developer CV) surfaced how much the resume parser had quietly been tuned to just one layout — six distinct parsing failures came out of it: section headings only recognized in ALL CAPS, a bare address line bleeding into the summary, a job's location mistaken for its title when it shared a line with the date, an entire job disappearing when it was written with no bullet points, a date range losing its separator on a multi-column layout, and bullets themselves having no stored order at all, so two uploads of the identical PDF could come back with a different bullet sequence. Beyond the specific patches, resume parsing now has an AI-based fallback that only runs when the free, deterministic pass comes back looking broken, rather than growing an ever-larger pile of one-off rules for every new layout.

The skill dictionary that powers gap analysis and fabrication-checking no longer has to be hand-written either — every resume upload now teaches it any genuinely new terms on its own. The same dictionary picked up two smaller, real fixes: AWS and EC2 stopped being treated as one identical skill (EC2 is one specific service, not the whole platform), and the skill badge now distinguishes a skill whose exact wording is in your resume from one that's only true through different phrasing — worth knowing since some real-world ATS software only matches literal keywords. Teaching that dictionary to also recognize soft-skill and job-title phrasing (for the badge) had a side effect worth naming: it quietly taught the separate fabrication-checker to flag things like "Full Stack Engineer" as an unproven claim. Fixed by excluding that specific handful of terms from what counts as a checkable claim at all, without weakening what it still catches for a genuinely fabricated technical skill.

Two more rounds of real-application testing surfaced further fixes and one new feature. Real recruiter feedback ("the exact job title wasn't found in your resume") led to a new per-application title suggestion: the JD's own title with a fixed list of seniority/scope words (Senior, Lead, Chief, Director, and similar) stripped out, since inflating a title the resume doesn't actually back up is exactly the kind of claim this project's guardrails exist to prevent elsewhere. The Job Details panel gained tabs to hold it alongside the existing JD/company view. A STAR-rephrase bug was also caught live: compressing "MongoDB, Express.js, React, Node.js" down to "the MERN stack" read as true but silently dropped a keyword ("Express.js") the JD was actually matching on — fixed by reverting a bullet to its verbatim source whenever a rephrase drops a skill that was both in the source and JD-matched. The tailored summary was separately found opening with a different role framing than the Title section showed a few lines below it (one inferred by the model, the other computed deterministically) — fixed by having the summary open with the actual suggested title instead of guessing its own.

Export gained a second file format: resumes and cover letters can now be downloaded as PDF as well as Word, converted from the exact same on-demand `.docx` buffer via LibreOffice rather than built independently, so the two formats can never drift apart. Filenames also changed from a generic "resume.docx" to a real, ATS-safe FirstName_LastName_Company pattern, since a filename with spaces or punctuation is a real, if minor, thing that trips up some ATS intake forms. The exported Word document itself picked up several formatting fixes found by actually opening generated files: a lighter bullet glyph, tighter default paragraph spacing (removing an unnecessary near-empty extra page), "Work History" replacing "Experience" as a more ATS-recognized heading, and a cleaner single-line Education entry layout.

The Approval page itself went through a visual overhaul: the flat "ATS score: N" text became a color-tiered radial gauge, and flags gained color-coded severity badges — display changes only, with no change to how the score or flags are computed or used in the retry logic. Its 1,300-line single file was also split into 17 focused components for readability, verified against a full live run through the pipeline with no behavior change. The page was then restructured again — a single Download menu replacing two separate dropdowns, hover-triggered info icons added at every spot a first-time user would otherwise have no way to decode (the ATS score, flags, rephrase %, retry count, the two checks below, and Re-check), and two new read-only panels: a skill-frequency comparison (how often each matched skill appears in the JD versus the resume) and a searchability check (contact info present, no header/footer elements, standard headings present — checked against the real generated export, not the input data). Three real bugs were also fixed along the way: a stale keyword-gap read in the skill-match bar, the add-skill input and Re-check button staying clickable after an application was already approved, and the sticky header's mobile layout.

The app also moved from a single-user tool to real multi-user authentication — signup/login with a JWT, a per-user skill-alias dictionary instead of one shared file, and ownership scoping across every resume and application endpoint — deliberately reversing the original "just me, for now" scope decision in favor of future scaling, with forgot/reset-password support included from the start.

A full QA pass across every layer of the app — auth, resume parsing, all 11 pipeline nodes, human-approval actions, export, and the UI — surfaced seven real bugs, since fixed. The most serious: `auth.js` was the one route file with no error handling at all, so a single malformed signup or login request (no valid account needed) could crash the entire server; three such payloads were confirmed live before the fix. Two bugs affected the pipeline's core trustworthiness: gap analysis could read a stale, un-translated version of the resume's own skills (a bullet clearly mentioning "AWS EC2" still showing as a gap), and the mandated "5+ years" phrasing in the tailored summary almost always failed its own fabrication check, which could silently block a legitimate retry for an unrelated reason. A related, everyday case was also fixed: a job posting spelling a skill the resume already has, but differently ("ReactJS" against a resume that only ever says "React"), had no way to ever be recognized, since the skill dictionary only ever learned from resume text, never from job postings — it now also learns from a job's own wording, conservatively, checking only against skills already confirmed on the resume rather than guessing freely. The remaining fixes: manual retries had no cap at all despite the documented limit of 3 (one test run reached 10 before it was caught); the tailored summary's "no first-person, no filler phrase" rule had no automatic check behind it, unlike the metric rule, and could still be violated; and the cover letter could name the candidate's own current job title as "the position," rather than the job actually being applied to.

The skill dictionary's growth mechanism, previously tied only to the full resume-upload flow, now teaches itself from every place a new skill term can actually be typed — an in-app master-resume bullet edit, a hand-edited application bullet or summary, or an accepted skill-gap suggestion all feed it now, not just an upload. Re-uploading a resume this app itself had exported (a realistic thing to do — starting fresh from a polished export) surfaced five more real bugs in one pass: a term the dictionary's own grouping call silently dropped (a missing "langgraph") now gets a guaranteed fallback; a genuinely fabricated skill ("unit testing") had wrongly been on the fabrication-check's exclusion list and was removed; the exported "WORK HISTORY" heading was reverted back to "EXPERIENCE" after discovering the upload parser never learned to recognize its own export's ATS-motivated synonym; a wrapped bullet with no trailing punctuation was being mistaken for a finished sentence, corrupting the next job's header; and the contact-info parser learned to split on the middle-dot character this app's own export actually writes, plus to reassemble a portfolio URL that PDF-wraps mid-hostname across two lines.

Gap analysis, Skill Frequency, and Re-check went through a round of consistency fixes after live testing found them disagreeing with each other on the same application. A JD's own required-skill list had the same "frozen at first extraction" staleness bug the resume side had already been fixed for — a skill re-canonicalized against a dictionary update was correctly picked up on the resume side but not the JD side, keeping some gaps falsely open. Separately, three features didn't agree on what "the resume" even means for matching purposes: the live keyword-gap count included Projects but not the tailored summary, Skill Frequency scanned neither, and Re-check scanned the summary but not Projects — visible as the "Skills Matched" count actually getting worse the moment Re-check ran, with nothing about the resume having changed. All three now read from the same live, non-persisted coverage computation. A related gap: a skill genuinely written in the tailored text, in its own literal wording, could still show as permanently missing if the AI tagger had only ever recorded a paraphrase of it — the Approval page now surfaces this as "found in your resume, not yet recognized," letting a one-click confirm teach the exact wording to the dictionary, or a dismiss silence a coincidental false match on just that one application.

The tailored summary's choice of which matched skills to actually name stopped being left to the tailoring model's own unguided judgment — a real GenAI-focused posting produced a summary opening with generic "React, TypeScript, and AWS" while skipping the LangChain/LangGraph experience that JD was actually about. A small, separate ranking step now judges which matched skills the JD's own framing (its title, overview, and repeated theme — not just checklist position) actually cares about most, and the summary now names 3–4 of them instead of 2–3.

The exported resume was benchmarked directly against one built with a real resume-building tool, rather than tuned by eye, which turned up several concrete gaps: unset page margins were spilling identical content onto an extra page, every job/degree/project heading was colored and sized like a section label rather than a subordinate one, a job's date sat on its own stacked line instead of aligned with its header (partly because the layout math assumed the wrong page size), fonts weren't declared explicitly enough for LibreOffice to render them consistently, and contact links weren't real clickable hyperlinks. All fixed, along with a job-header parsing bug where a company name coincidentally containing a job-title word (e.g. "Developer" inside "Independent Developer") could wrongly discard the real parsed role.

Two smaller quality-of-life pieces landed alongside all of the above: the applications list moved from fetching and filtering the whole history client-side to real server-side pagination, and an already-approved application that never originally requested a cover letter can now generate one on demand rather than requiring a whole new application.

Remaining work is mostly refinement rather than net-new pipeline stages — see the workflow document for anything still open.
