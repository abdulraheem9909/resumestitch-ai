# Key Decisions Log

A running record of the architectural choices behind ResumeStitch AI and why they were made — for quick reference, so the reasoning doesn't have to be re-derived from scratch in a future session. Each entry names what we chose and, briefly, what it was chosen over.

**Retrieval, not generation, for tailoring.** Resume content lives as individual `resumeBullets` records; the tailoring node selects and rephrases from them rather than writing from a blank page. Chosen over free-form generation because it removes most of the surface area for fabrication structurally, rather than relying on the model to police itself.

**Deterministic verification as its own node, before scoring.** A dedicated node checks every tailored claim against its source bullet, separate from and running before ATS scoring. Chosen because "is this true" and "does this score well" are different questions — folding them together lets a resume that reads well but overstates something slip through unnoticed.

**Retry policy driven by named flags, not a raw ATS score.** The loop only retries on specific problems (missing requirement, unsupported claim, poor readability); a low score alone doesn't trigger a retry. Chosen over threshold-based retry because optimizing straight at an LLM-estimated score rewards keyword stuffing rather than genuine improvement.

**Role fit gate before tailoring.** A cheap overlap check (plus an LLM call only for ambiguous cases) exits early when a JD and resume are a poor match — e.g. a recruiter JD against a software-engineer resume — before any tailoring or scoring spend. Chosen because without it, a bad-fit JD would still burn three full retry cycles for no benefit, since there's no more relevant material to surface on attempt three than attempt one.

**The role fit gate's low-overlap floor was removed — a numeric overlap percentage can't structurally distinguish wrong-stack from wrong-discipline.** Node 4 originally auto-rejected (no LLM call at all) whenever canonical skill overlap fell below 20%, in addition to the ≥50% free fast-pass. Chosen after finding, via live testing against five real postings against an actual uploaded resume, that a same-discipline JD with a different tech stack produces the identical signal as a genuinely cross-discipline one: a Java/Spring backend JD scored a flat 0% overlap against a Node.js/React resume and was auto-rejected, and a Node.js/React/Python JD — literally naming that resume's own top two skills — scored 19%, one point under the floor, and was auto-rejected without ever reaching the stage-2 check that already judges this distinction correctly. No floor value fixes this, because it isn't a mistuned number — a low-but-same-discipline overlap and a genuinely cross-discipline overlap are indistinguishable from the raw percentage alone, so there's no floor value strictly between "still makes this mistake" and "removed entirely." A denominator reweight (e.g. top-N skills, core-vs-nice-to-have weighting) doesn't fix it either, since it doesn't change that the compared skill tokens are simply different words — closing that gap would mean hand-building a discipline taxonomy that duplicates, with less confidence, exactly what the stage-2 prompt already does correctly. Chosen to remove the floor entirely rather than retune it: only the ≥50% fast-*pass* remains free, since that direction is safe — high overlap can never itself indicate a discipline mismatch — while everything else, including 0% or unmeasurable overlap, now defers to the stage-2 LLM call. Accepted trade-off: most real submissions that aren't a slam-dunk match now cost one extra cheap gpt-4o-mini call instead of being free when they're a bad fit.

**Human approval as the final gate, with AI/human/final text kept distinct.** Nothing is saved without explicit review, and the state model tracks what the model generated, what was hand-edited, and what was actually approved as three separate fields. Chosen so it's always possible to tell what came from the AI and what came from me, rather than losing that distinction the moment an edit is made.

**Resume parsing lives outside the per-JD pipeline entirely.** Uploading and parsing a resume into bullets is a one-time operation; a LangGraph thread only ever reads already-parsed bullets, never parses a file itself. Chosen over parsing-per-run because parsing is expensive and unnecessary to repeat, and because it's what made clean support for multiple resumes possible.

**Multiple master resumes, capped at 5, with a full cascade on delete.** Deleting a resume removes every linked application, generated file, and thread history before the resume itself is removed. Chosen to keep the resume list manageable and to avoid orphaned data referencing a resume that no longer exists.

**In-app bullet editing is a direct, in-place update.** Editing a bullet changes that database record directly, with no versioning or history kept. Chosen for simplicity over an additive/versioned approach (keep the old bullet, add a new one, mark the old deprecated) — the tradeoff, made knowingly: an old application's `sourceBulletId` can end up pointing at wording that's since changed, so it can't be used as a perfect historical record of what a past application actually claimed.

**No batching, no queue — one JD processed at a time.** Batch submission and BullMQ-based parallel processing were designed, then deliberately removed. Chosen because this is a single-person tool, not a multi-tenant service, and the simplicity was worth more than the throughput.

**Paste JD text directly; no URL fetching.** The pipeline never fetches a job posting server-side — the JD is pasted in, along with a company name and an optional, never-fetched reference link for personal recall later. Chosen over URL scraping because pasted text is more accurate (no JS-rendering gaps, no login walls, no scraped nav/footer noise) and removes an entire category of risk — SSRF exposure and job-board ToS issues — that scraping would have introduced.

**Skill matching runs on a canonicalized taxonomy, not raw text.** JD and resume skill terms are normalized against a controlled alias dictionary (e.g. "Node.js" / "Node" / "NodeJS" → one canonical ID) before any gap analysis. Chosen because a raw set difference produces false gaps from surface-form mismatches alone, which would otherwise tempt the tailoring step to "fill" gaps that were never really there.

**Every node is idempotent; every generation is versioned.** LLM results are cached by `(applicationId, nodeName, inputHash, promptVersion, model)`, and every generation records the resume version, prompt version, and model used. Chosen because the combination of retries, checkpointing, and LLM calls makes accidental double-execution likely without it, and because prompt/model changes are otherwise impossible to distinguish from resume changes months later.

**Re-checking a hand-edited resume stores its own score, separate from the AI's.** Editing the tailored text yourself doesn't automatically re-score it — a non-blocking "re-check" action does that on request, and the result (`humanRecheckAtsScore`/`Flags`) is kept apart from the AI-generated `atsScore`/`atsFlags` rather than overwriting them. Chosen for the same reason `generatedText` and `humanEditedText` are kept separate elsewhere: losing which score belongs to which version of the text would make the record less trustworthy, not more convenient.

**Skill suggestions during approval add a real resume bullet — never insert text directly into the tailored output.** When a JD wants a skill you have real experience with but never added to your resume, accepting the suggestion routes through the same one-time bullet-creation flow used for uploads (write the bullet, tag it, save it to `resumeBullets`), then re-tailors from there. Chosen over direct insertion because that shortcut would recreate the exact fabrication risk every other guardrail in this pipeline exists to prevent — content with no `sourceBulletId`, unverifiable, and indistinguishable from something the model invented.

**Export generates files on demand, not as an automatic graph side effect.** The original node 11 design wrote `.docx`/`tracker.xlsx` automatically once a run reached a terminal state. In practice this was replaced with `GET` routes that build the file fresh from MongoDB whenever it's requested, gated on `status === 'approved'`, and never persist it server-side. Chosen because on-demand generation is simpler to keep correct — there's no separate "exported" state to fall out of sync with the underlying data, and re-downloading after a later edit just reflects whatever is current in Mongo rather than a stale file from whenever node 11 last ran.

**A retry re-derives the resume's skill list from current bullets, not a snapshot from the first pass.** Both the manual and suggest-missing-skills retry paths route back through gap analysis before re-tailoring, recomputing `resumeCanonicalSkills`/`keywordGaps` from whatever `resumeBullets` currently holds. Chosen after finding that a bullet added mid-flow (e.g. to plug a JD gap) had no effect on the displayed gap list, since gap analysis previously only ever ran once, on the very first pass — silently stale from that point on regardless of what changed afterward.

**A hand-edited bullet or summary survives a retry instead of being overwritten by the fresh regeneration.** Retrying re-runs the tailoring model, which naturally produces entirely new text for every bullet. Anything with `editSource: "human"` from the previous round is now carried forward as-is rather than replaced. Chosen because silently discarding a review edit the moment another retry happens would violate the same "AI/human/final text kept distinct" guarantee this pipeline already promises elsewhere — an edit made to fix a real problem (e.g. a fabricated-skill flag) has no business disappearing just because an unrelated retry ran afterward.

**The summary half of that guarantee only actually worked for bullets, not summaries — fixed by re-syncing `tailoredSummary` into the graph on retry, the same way `tailoredBullets` already was.** `PATCH /:id/summary` writes a hand-edit straight to the Mongo `applications` document, but never touched the LangGraph checkpoint's own `state.tailoredSummary`. `tailorContentNode`'s preservation check (`state.tailoredSummary?.editSource === 'human' ? state.tailoredSummary : generated.tailoredSummary`) was correctly written, but every retry path (`POST /:id/resume`, `POST /:id/suggest-skills/accept`) only ever re-synced `tailoredBullets` from Mongo into the resume payload — never `tailoredSummary` — so the graph's own state never learned a human had touched it, and the check always saw stale pre-edit state. Found via live testing: hand-editing a summary, then accepting a suggest-missing-skills bullet (an unrelated, legitimate retry trigger), silently reverted the summary to a fresh AI generation with no error. Chosen to fix by including `tailoredSummary: application.tailoredSummary` in both resume-Command payloads and having `humanApprovalNode`'s retry branch pass it through (`resumeValue.tailoredSummary ?? state.tailoredSummary`) — mirroring exactly what already worked for `tailoredBullets`, rather than inventing a new mechanism.

**A bullet added to plug a JD skill gap is force-included verbatim, not left to the model's discretion.** Accepting a suggest-missing-skills bullet only ever nudged the tailoring model with a soft note ("consider incorporating this if relevant") — the model remained free to drop it, and did. Chosen to instead deterministically append that specific bullet's original text if the model's own selection doesn't include it, since the entire reason it exists is to close that gap; leaving it to chance defeats the point of adding it. Verbatim inclusion (no extra LLM call) also means zero additional fabrication risk.

**Bullet selection guarantees at least one bullet per real employer.** Node 5 originally selected "the 3-6 most relevant bullets" from the whole resume as one flat pool, with no rule protecting employer coverage — which let an entire job disappear from a tailored resume if none of its bullets ranked among the top few for a given JD. Chosen to deterministically add back the highest-JD-overlap bullet for any employer left unrepresented, because an unexplained employment gap reads far worse on a real resume than one moderately-relevant bullet would.

**Node 5 keeps every candidate bullet by default and only excludes one judged genuinely out of context, instead of selecting a top-N subset.** The tailoring model previously picked "around 15-16 relevant bullets" out of the full candidate pool — anything not picked simply vanished, with no record it was ever considered. Chosen to flip the default: the model now returns a `rejected` verdict for every single candidate bullet, defaulting to keep, and reserves `rejected: true` for a bullet that's genuinely irrelevant to the JD's domain, not merely weaker than another candidate. Rejected bullets still flow into `tailoredBullets` (verbatim, `rephraseIntensity: 0`) so the human reviewer can see exactly what got excluded and why — tagged, read-only, on the Approval page — while every downstream node (cover letter, style lint, ATS scoring, the verification summary check) filters them out before use, and the `.docx` export filters them out before the file is built. This also meant the two existing safety nets (force-included skill-gap bullet, guaranteed employer coverage) had to change from "append if missing" to "un-reject in place if present but rejected," since the candidate pool no longer omits anything.

**The skills list is editable in two independent scopes, and stays purely cosmetic either way.** `masterResumes.skills` can be edited permanently (the master-resume edit dialog); `applications.tailoredSkills` is an optional per-application override of that same list, scoped to just one export, falling back to the live master list when unset — the same "override with fallback" shape as `tailoredBullets`/`tailoredSummary`. Neither ever feeds gap analysis, ATS scoring, or fabrication verification — confirmed by tracing every consumer of `skills`. Chosen because letting an edit here move the score or close a keyword gap would be the exact shortcut every other guardrail in this pipeline exists to prevent: typing a word into a list and getting uncontested credit for it. Each chip is additionally color-coded — solid if the skill is genuinely found in real bullet text (`computeVerifiedSkills`, reusing the same text-scanning matcher already trusted for fabrication checks), outline if it's merely self-declared — but this signal is display-only too, never fed back into anything upstream.

**Resume-upload contact fields are deterministically pre-filled from the file, but never auto-saved.** Selecting a file now runs a rule-based (no LLM) scan of the document's first few lines — name, phone, email, LinkedIn/portfolio URLs, a title tagline — and pre-populates the upload form's fields, plus a suggested label. Chosen over leaving every field blank because most of this is copy-able straight off the page with simple pattern matching, and chosen over an LLM call because it's cheap, deterministic, and this is a one-time upload-time convenience, not something worth spending a model call on. Every field stays a normal editable input and nothing is written to `masterResumes` until the user reviews and submits — this only changes what the blank form starts with, never what "you approve everything" means.

**Deterministic section-parsing gained two layout tolerances it didn't originally have.** Uploading a real resume (the developer's own) surfaced two bugs: a resume with no explicit `SUMMARY`-style heading lost its summary paragraph entirely, and a resume with "degree name on its own line, institution+location+date together on the next line" (rather than "degree+date share a line, institution follows") had its degree and institution fields silently swapped and shifted between entries. Fixed by adding an implied-summary fallback (preamble prose left over once name/contact-line/tagline-line patterns are excluded, used only when no explicit heading is ever found) and a second education-parsing branch keyed on which line the date actually shares. Chosen over requiring a specific resume template, since the whole point of file upload is accepting a resume as it already exists, not making the user reformat it to satisfy the parser.

**The tailored summary never states an achievement metric at all — only the years-of-experience figure.** Two earlier, softer rules were tried and both failed empirically against a live retry: "include exactly one metric" and "don't restate a number that's already in a bullet" both still produced a summary repeating two or three numbers verbatim from the bullets shown right below it. The realization: in this retrieval-only pipeline, every metric available to the summary necessarily also lives in a bullet, since bullets are the *only* place a metric can come from — so a conditional "unless it's already in a bullet" rule is nearly always vacuously true, and asking the model to self-verify that condition during generation is exactly the kind of soft instruction it doesn't reliably comply with. Replacing the conditional with a flat prohibition (no metric at all, ever, except years-of-experience) is what actually stopped the repetition when tested live — confirmed by retrying the same real application against each prompt version and reading the result back from the generation cache, not by inspecting the prompt text alone.

**The skill-alias dictionary is now grown by AI at resume-upload time, instead of hand-written.** `server/data/skillAliases.json` started as a fixed, manually-maintained list of synonyms ("Node"/"NodeJS"/"Node.js" → one canonical id). That doesn't scale past the one stack it was written against — uploading a resume for a different domain entirely (tested live with a security/door-supervisor CV) meant every one of its skill terms was invisible to gap analysis and to the deterministic fabrication check, since neither can recognize a term with zero entries. Considered generating aliases fresh on every use instead of persisting them — rejected, because it would mean re-deriving the same fixed fact (e.g. "Node" = "Node.js") on every single run, at real cost, with a real chance an LLM call answers slightly differently between runs, undermining the exact determinism the fabrication check depends on. Chosen instead: after a resume introduces genuinely new terms, one batched call proposes canonical groupings, merged in with a hard safety rule — never overwrite an alias that already points elsewhere, and redirect a proposed canonical id that's itself an existing alias to its real target, so two competing "true names" for one concept can never coexist. Computed once, at upload (already a one-time cost per resume), then reused for free on every future gap-analysis/verification call — not re-derived per JD.

**`canonicalizeSkill.js`/`deterministicVerification.js` read the alias dictionary through one shared, reloadable store, not their own independent copies.** Both used to `readFileSync` the same file at their own module load — meaning a write during upload (see above) would never be seen by the already-running server process without a restart. Chosen to centralize loading behind `skillAliasesStore.js`, with an explicit reload called right after every write, so a newly-learned alias is visible to the very next request. The same store also had to stop crashing the server outright when the dictionary file doesn't exist yet (a fresh clone, or the very first-ever upload with no file at all) — it now starts with an empty dictionary and the file is created on first write, rather than throwing at startup.

**"AWS" and "EC2" are separate canonical skills, not one merged id.** They used to share one canonical id, which meant a resume that only ever said "AWS" got credit for an "Amazon EC2" skill badge with zero evidence EC2 specifically was ever used. EC2 is one specific AWS service (virtual servers), not the whole platform — treating them as identical was a pre-existing, un-scrutinized dictionary entry, not a new decision, until this narrowing found it.

**The verified-skill badge distinguishes an exact wording match from a same-meaning-different-wording match.** Previously a skill either showed as backed by your bullets or not, with no way to tell "the literal word is there" from "only a synonym of it is there" (e.g. a "Node.js" badge backed by bullet text that only ever says "Node"). Worth surfacing because this app's own matching is deliberately synonym-aware (see above), but some real-world ATS software at actual employers is not — it does literal keyword scanning. Chosen to add a second, "partial" match state (checked by testing the skill's own literal text against the source, separately from the existing alias-based check) so the difference is visible without changing what counts as verified at all, and without touching gap analysis or scoring, which stay synonym-aware on purpose (see "don't optimize toward the wrong number").

**The deterministic anti-fabrication check excludes a fixed, short list of soft-skill/job-title canonical ids from what counts as a checkable "claim."** Teaching the alias dictionary to recognize terms like "Mentorship," "Code Review," and "Full-Stack Engineer" (so the verified-skill badge could credit them) had a side effect: the same recognition feeds the fabrication check, which compares "skills a bullet claims" against skills that were actually part of JD-matching — a vocabulary that deliberately never includes job titles or soft skills in the first place (`tagBullet.js`'s own prompt excludes them). Recognizing one of these terms as a claim therefore made it permanently unprovable, and a real application's tailored summary restating its own job title ("...as a Full Stack Engineer...") was flagged as a fabricated skill. Considered filtering these terms out of the badge's recognition instead — rejected, since that would just resurrect the original bug the recognition was added to fix. Chosen instead: a short, explicit, hand-maintained exclusion list used only by the fabrication check, never by the badge — so a term stays recognized for display purposes but is never treated as something requiring proof. Deliberately not a broad pattern-based rule, so a genuine hard skill can never be swept into the exclusion by accident; backed by a test asserting a real fabricated hard skill is still caught exactly as before.

**Resume parsing gained an AI-based fallback, used only when the deterministic pass looks broken, rather than another hand-written rule per new layout.** Testing against two resumes with layouts genuinely different from the one this parser was originally tuned against (a security/hospitality CV, a Flutter developer CV) surfaced six separate failures at once: bullet glyphs that don't survive PDF text extraction (producing zero bullets — a resume with none can't even be used to create an application), a date range losing its connecting dash to a multi-column layout, section headings only recognized in ALL CAPS, a bare location line bleeding into the summary, a job's location mistaken for its role/company when it shared a line with the date, and a job written with no bullet points at all silently vanishing even while sitting next to properly-bulleted ones. The cheap, safe fixes (a looser date regex, Title-Case headings, recognizing a location line, preferring an already-complete header buffered above a location that shares the date line) were applied directly. For the rest — genuinely no bullet marker left in the text at all, or a section's content scattered by column ordering — no new regex could safely generalize without risking a false match on some other, correctly-formatted resume. Chosen instead: keep the free, deterministic pass as the default for every resume (near-zero cost, and it's already correct most of the time), and only when it comes back looking clearly broken — zero bullets, an empty or visibly wrong education/skills result, an oversized summary, or fewer distinct jobs found than date-range lines in the text — ask an AI to find the same boundaries, copying bullet/section text out verbatim rather than generating anything. No fabrication risk either way: the AI is only ever asked where the boundaries are, never to invent content, since the actual text still comes from the document either way. Found live that the job-recovery fallback was itself non-deterministic on a first pass (it silently dropped a lightly-described job under a secondary "Other Experience" heading roughly half the time) — fixed by making the prompt state explicitly that a brief, secondary, or non-bulleted job counts exactly as much as a fully-bulleted one, then confirmed 5/5 repeat calls before trusting it.

**Every `resumeBullets` document now stores its own position, and every route that lists them sorts by it.** Bullets never had a stored order at all — display order depended entirely on MongoDB's own read order for a collection, which is not a guaranteed match for insertion order. Found live: two uploads of the identical PDF came back with the same bullets in a different sequence. Chosen to add an explicit `order` field, populated at every creation site (initial upload, in that document's own order; a manually added bullet, or one added via a suggest-missing-skills bullet, always appended after whatever's already there, never at the front) — with every route that reads bullets back now sorting by it. Deliberately does not retroactively fix a resume uploaded before this change: its bullets simply have no meaningful position yet, so they keep displaying in whatever order they currently do rather than having a new, still-arbitrary order silently imposed on them.

**A per-application resume title (`tailoredTitle`) is suggested from the JD's own title, minus a fixed seniority/scope word list — never generated, and never verified.** Real recruiter feedback flagged a JD's exact title as "not found in your resume," and separately, a genuine multi-role situation came up: the same underlying full-stack/AI work legitimately supports being framed as a Software Engineer, Full-Stack Engineer, Backend Engineer, or AI Engineer depending on which JD it's aimed at, but the master resume's tagline can only say one of those at a time. Considered generating a title with an LLM call — rejected, since a title is exactly the kind of claim ("you are a Senior X") that's easy to accidentally inflate, and this pipeline's whole design already avoids letting the model invent anything about seniority or scope. Considered copying the JD's title verbatim — rejected on its own, since a JD title can itself claim a seniority/scope level ("Senior", "Staff", "Lead", "Principal", "Founding", "Head of", "Chief", "VP", "Director") the resume doesn't back up. Chosen instead: a small deterministic function (`suggestResumeTitle.js`) that strips that fixed, hand-maintained word list from the JD's own title text — the same "explicit short list, not a broad pattern" precedent already used for `NON_VERIFIABLE_CLAIM_IDS` — falling back to the resume's existing title unchanged if nothing usable is left. Because it only ever removes words from real, already-known text (or falls back to the resume's own existing title), there's no fabrication surface at all, so node 6 doesn't verify it, for the same reason `tailoredSkills` doesn't either. Given the same `{generatedText, humanEditedText, finalText, editSource}` shape as `tailoredSummary`, including the identical retry-preservation behavior (this time built correctly from the start, unlike `tailoredSummary`'s own resync bug documented above) — confirmed live by hand-editing a suggested title, retrying, and seeing the edit survive. Also newly threaded into node 9's ATS/recruiter prompt as `<resume_title>`, since that node previously never saw the resume's title at all and couldn't judge title-to-role alignment as part of its score/feedback — confirmed live: a JD titled "Lead Backend Engineer" against a resume with no leadership bullets produced a suggested title of "Backend Engineer" (the honest, unsupported "Lead" dropped) and recruiter feedback that correctly asked for leadership evidence instead of a generic title-mismatch complaint.

**A hand-edited title gets one narrow, informational-only seniority-word warning — not the same fabrication check bullets/summary get, and not extended to catch domain fabrication either.** A hand-edited `tailoredTitle` bypasses the deterministic fabrication check entirely, same as a hand-edited bullet or summary (`trustHumanEdit()` always passes) — that's deliberate and unchanged, since a human edit is your own claim about yourself, not the AI's. But unlike bullets/summary, title already has a fixed, exact, hand-maintained list of words we know this resume can't back up (`SENIORITY_QUALIFIER_TERMS`, the same list the AI suggestion strips from the JD title). Considered leaving this alone, matching bullets/summary exactly — rejected, since re-checking a hand-edited title against a list we already maintain for the AI path is essentially free and catches a real, concrete risk (typing "Chief" or "Director" back into a title the AI had deliberately trimmed). Considered extending this into a general title-fabrication check (e.g. flagging a hand-edited title claiming an unsupported domain like "Machine Learning Engineer") — rejected for now as a materially bigger feature: it would require comparing the title's implied domain against canonical skills actually present in the kept bullets, not a word-list lookup, and wasn't what was asked for. Chosen: `findUnsupportedSeniorityTerms()` reuses the exact same word list, runs only against a hand-edited title (the AI-generated one can never contain one of these words by construction), and surfaces as a plain inline warning next to the title on the Approval page — never blocking the save, computed live on every `GET /:id` (not just right after the edit) so it survives a page reload, the same "compute live, never persist" pattern already used for `verifiedSkills`/`skillMatchTypes`.

**The Approval page now tracks whether the on-screen score/feedback is stale relative to your last edit, and prompts you to Re-check.** Before this, nothing distinguished "the score you're looking at reflects what's currently saved" from "you just toggled a bullet's include/exclude checkbox and the score hasn't caught up yet" — Re-check has always been a manual, on-request action, so a stale score could sit on screen indefinitely with no visual difference from a current one. Chosen: a purely client-side `dirtySinceCheck` flag, set the moment any edit/include-exclude action saves successfully (bullet text, bullet include/exclude, summary, title, or skills), and cleared either by running Re-check or by a retry ("send back with notes"), since a retry already re-runs the same human-recheck computation on your behalf as part of that flow — confirmed by tracing that `sendBackWithNotes` and `acceptSuggestedSkill` both route through the same `loadApplication()` reload this flag also resets on. Surfaced as a fixed-position blue info toast in the bottom corner of the viewport (not an inline card banner) with its own "Re-check edited text" action button, so it stays visible and actionable regardless of how far you've scrolled down the page — deliberately styled as a distinct "info" color rather than reusing the destructive/error red already used elsewhere on this page, since a stale score is a normal, expected state to pass through, not a problem. No backend change needed at all, since this is purely about the state of the page you're looking at, not a new fact about the application itself.

**A fourth node-5 deterministic guarantee reverts a bullet to its verbatim source text if the model's own rephrase dropped a JD-matched skill that was literally in the original.** Found on a real application: a bullet explicitly listing "MongoDB, Express.js, React, Node.js" got STAR-rephrased down to "the MERN stack" — true in meaning, but "MERN" isn't a registered alias for `express` specifically, so the literal word vanished from the exported text. The keyword-gap list then correctly reported `express` as missing, per its own documented job (it only ever looks at what's actually in the exported file) — that part wasn't the bug. The actual gap: node 5's only hard rule was "never invent a skill absent from the source," with nothing stopping it from removing one that was already there. Considered leaving this to the prompt (an instruction like "don't drop a mentioned skill") — rejected for the same reason force-including a skill-gap bullet and guaranteeing employer coverage were both made deterministic rather than left to model compliance: this is a concretely checkable condition, and a soft instruction isn't reliable enough for something checkable. Chosen: `preserveMatchedSkillWording()` compares canonical skills extracted from a kept bullet's source text against its tailored text (reusing `extractClaimedSkills`, already used the same way in `verifiedSkills.js`); if a skill is in both the source text and `matchedSkills` (the JD∩resume overlap) but missing from the tailored text, the bullet reverts to its verbatim source — the same safe, zero-fabrication-risk fallback the other two node-5 guarantees already use, rather than trying to surgically re-insert one word into an arbitrary sentence. Deliberately scoped to `matchedSkills` only (an irrelevant dropped word doesn't matter) and skips any bullet with `editSource: "human"` (a human's own rewording is never second-guessed), matching the same rules the other guarantees already follow.

**The tailored summary now opens with the actual `tailoredTitle`, instead of a role phrase the model infers on its own from the bullets.** Found live: the Title section could show "Senior Software Engineer" while the Summary a few lines below opened with "...as a Fullstack & AI Engineer" — two independent framings of the same resume, because summary generation never actually received the tailored title as an input at all; it only ever saw the kept bullets, matched skills, and years of experience. Chosen fix: compute `tailoredTitle` *before* calling the tailoring model (it never depended on that call's output anyway — only on `jdTitle`/`resumeTitle`/a prior human edit, all already available earlier), then pass its `finalText` in as a fourth input the summary must open with verbatim, replacing the old "role implied by the kept bullets" instruction. `computeInputHash` and `TAILOR_PROMPT_VERSION` (bumped to `tailor-v11`) both updated so a title change correctly invalidates the cache and old cached generations made without this input aren't silently reused. Because title is computed first and a hand-edited title already wins over a fresh AI suggestion, a hand-edited title now also becomes what the next retry's summary opens with — not just what the Title section displays — for free, with no separate wiring needed.

**Removed the static resume-preview tagline once the Title section could show the same thing, plus more.** The read-only "Your resume" card at the top of the Approval page used to show `masterResume.personalInfo.title` as a standalone line above the candidate's name. Once the dedicated Title section existed (Original/Tailored/Edit), that line became pure duplication — the Title section already shows the exact same "Original" value, plus the tailored one. Removed rather than kept as a fallback; an application created before `tailoredTitle` existed simply shows no title indicator until its next retry, the same "not worth a backfill for a self-healing gap" tradeoff already made for `ResumeBullet.order`.

**Resume and cover letter exports are now also available as PDF, converted from the same on-demand `.docx` buffer via LibreOffice headless — not a second, independently-built file.** `GET /:id/export/resume.pdf` and `/cover-letter.pdf` call the identical docx-buffer builders already used by the `.docx` routes, then pipe the result through `soffice --headless --convert-to pdf` (`convertDocxToPdf.js`) and return the converted bytes. Nothing is written to disk beyond one per-request temp directory, always removed in a `finally` block regardless of success or failure — the same "never stored server-side" rule the `.docx` routes already followed. Chosen over a separate PDF-specific document builder (e.g. laying out `pdf-lib` independently) because that would mean maintaining two divergent layouts for the same content, with a real risk of them drifting apart; converting the one already-approved-and-gated `.docx` buffer guarantees the PDF always matches the `.docx` exactly, and reuses the same `status === 'approved'`/`coverLetterRequested` gating as-is.

**Export filenames now follow a fixed `FirstName_LastName_CompanyName_<Resume|CoverLetter>.<ext>` pattern, built at request time and stripped of spaces/special characters, rather than a generic `resume.docx`-style name.** Real ATS intake forms and email attachment handling commonly mishandle or auto-rename a filename containing spaces or punctuation. Chosen to derive the filename from data already on hand (`personalInfo.fullName`, `applications.companyName`) at the moment of download rather than persisting a filename anywhere on the application document — consistent with `resumeFilename`/`coverLetterFilename` staying unpopulated since node 11's on-demand export redesign.

**The exported `.docx` picked up several formatting fixes, found by opening actual generated files rather than by reading the `docx` library's docs.** Bullets were rendering as a heavy 11pt "●" (BLACK CIRCLE) glyph, because the `bullet: { level: 0 }` shorthand has no way to size the glyph independently of body text — replaced with an explicit numbering definition using a lighter "•" at a smaller size and a tighter hanging indent. Word's own default "Normal" style carries roughly 10pt spacing-after on every paragraph, which — multiplied across dozens of bullets and lines — was what actually pushed a resume onto a near-empty extra page, not any single section on its own; fixed by lowering the document-wide default paragraph spacing and giving headings their own spacing back explicitly, so sections still read as visually separated. The Education entry layout changed from three visually disconnected lines (a degree+institution heading, an italic date line, a location line) to one subline combining institution, date range, and location together, matching the single-subline shape Experience entries already used. The "EXPERIENCE" heading became "WORK HISTORY", a standard ATS-recognized synonym, confirmed against real ATS-scanner feedback. Skills stayed a single flowing paragraph (Word's own natural line-wrap) after a fixed-row-count layout was tried and reverted — skill-name lengths vary too much to wrap evenly at a fixed count, producing an orphaned short row whenever the total isn't a clean multiple of the row size.

**"WORK HISTORY" was reverted back to "EXPERIENCE" as the exported resume's section heading — the ATS-synonym swap (see the exported-`.docx`-formatting entry above) turned out to create a real, separate bug of its own.** The upload parser's list of recognized Experience-section heading names (`resumeSectionHeadings.js`) was never updated to also accept "WORK HISTORY" — it still only knows "Experience"/"Work Experience"/"Professional Experience"/"Employment History"/"Other Experience"/"Additional Experience". Found live: re-uploading a resume that had itself been downloaded from this app (as a new master resume — a realistic thing to do, e.g. to start fresh from a polished export) failed to recognize its own "WORK HISTORY" heading as the Experience section at all, since `isSectionHeading()` correctly flags it as *a* heading (it's all-caps) but `classifySectionHeading()` returns `null` for it — contributing to a garbled, duplicated re-parse of that file. Considered fixing this by adding "WORK HISTORY" to the parser's recognized `EXPERIENCE_NAMES` instead, keeping the ATS-motivated heading — rejected: the user's own resume, and most resumes generally, already say "Experience," so there's no real need to introduce a second wording that then has to be kept in sync between the export writer and the upload parser forever. Chosen instead: revert `exportResumeDocx.js` back to writing "EXPERIENCE," removing the asymmetry at its source rather than teaching the parser a new synonym. The searchability check's `expectedHeadings` list (`applications.js`, section 4b) was updated to match, since it was asserting the exported file contains "WORK HISTORY" literally.

**Two new Approval-page panels, skill frequency and the searchability check, are purely informational and read-only — same guarantee already established for Coverage check and the verified-skill badges: neither feeds gap analysis, ATS scoring, fabrication verification, or the retry conditional.** Skill frequency (`skillFrequency.js`, `GET /:id/skill-frequency`) counts how many times each matched canonical skill — and any of its known aliases, via the same dictionary `canonicalizeSkill.js`/`verifiedSkills.js` already use — appears in the JD text versus the resume bullet text, surfacing a case like "verified, but the JD says it three times and the resume says it once" without changing what counts as a match anywhere else. Searchability check (`GET /:id/searchability-check`) rebuilds the current resume export in memory and checks three concrete ATS-parsing risks against the actual generated bytes: contact info present in the extracted document text, no header/footer XML parts used (some ATS parsers silently drop header/footer content), and the standard section headings actually present. Chosen to check the real exported `.docx` fresh on every call rather than a static rule about the input data, since what actually matters is whether the file an ATS receives will parse correctly, not just whether the input data looks right.

**A full QA pass across every layer of the app (auth, resume parsing, all 11 pipeline nodes, human-approval actions, export, and the UI) surfaced seven real bugs, all since fixed — recorded here as one batch since they were found and fixed together.**

**`auth.js` was the one route file with no `try/catch` anywhere, and a rejected promise there crashed the whole server, not just the request.** Every other route file already wraps its handlers (`applications.js` has 22 `try/catch` blocks, `resumes.js` has 20); `auth.js` had none, and under Express 4 an unhandled async rejection never reaches the error middleware — it takes the process down. Three unauthenticated payloads reproduced this live: a whitespace-only `fullName` on signup (the existing `!fullName` check doesn't catch a non-empty string of spaces), a `{"$ne":null}`-shaped `email` object on signup (the duplicate-check query safely coerces it, but the actual `User.create()` call doesn't), and the same shape for `password` on login against any real, self-registered email — reachable by anyone, since signup itself is open. Chosen fix: `typeof`/`.trim()` guards in front of every route's existing validation (fixing the actual root cause, since these are legitimate 400s, not 500s), plus `try/catch` around every DB/bcrypt call matching the convention already used everywhere else, plus the same `err.code === 11000` TOCTOU-race handling `applications.js` already uses for its own duplicate-key race. `forgot-password` specifically swallows a mail-delivery failure inside its own nested `try/catch` so the response stays identical either way, preserving the no-enumeration guarantee.

**Node 3 (gap analysis) was reading each bullet's stored `canonicalSkills` field verbatim, which could be permanently stale relative to the dictionary.** `ResumeBullet.canonicalSkills` is computed once at upload time using whichever aliases already existed *before* that same upload's own 2.1a learning step ran — for a first-time user this meant the field could be saved as raw, un-translated text (`"aws ec2"`) instead of the true canonical id (`"aws-ec2"`), and node 3 never re-derived it on any later pass, unlike node 2, which already re-canonicalizes the JD side live on every run. Found live: `keywordGaps` listed `aws-ec2` as missing on a resume whose bullet literally said "AWS EC2" twice, while the skill-frequency panel (which reads the dictionary correctly) simultaneously reported it present — two features on the same Approval page disagreeing about the same fact. Chosen fix: split the node into a pure, testable `computeGapAnalysis(state, aliases)` plus a thin async wrapper that fetches the current dictionary and re-runs the resume side's raw `canonicalSkills` values back through it before deduping — safe because re-canonicalizing an already-correct id is a no-op, so this can only ever turn a stale false "missing" into a correct "present," never the reverse.

**A JD spelling that never appears on any resume has no alias to close a real gap with, since the dictionary only ever grew from resume-side text.** Even after the fix above, a JD saying "ReactJS" against a resume that only ever says "React" still showed as missing, because "reactjs" had simply never been taught to anyone's dictionary — there was no stale value to re-resolve, just no entry at all. Considered a live, non-persisting check that never touches the dictionary — rejected, because the exact same check would then have to be repeated, at the same cost, on every future JD with the same phrasing, instead of the app ever actually learning it. Chosen: once per application (not repeated on retries), any still-unresolved JD gap is checked against the resume's own confirmed canonical skills via `matchUnresolvedSkillsToKnown()` — deliberately a narrower, closed-choice task than 2.1a's open-ended grouping: the model may only pick one of the *given* resume skill ids or "no match" (enforced via a dynamically-built `z.enum`, not just prompted for), since a wrong answer here directly turns a genuine gap into a false "already covered" credit rather than just leaving two spellings unmerged. A confirmed match is persisted through the same `addSkillAliasEntriesForUser`/`mergeAliasEntries` safety net 2.1a already uses, and this pass's own gaps are recomputed immediately. Also tightened `proposeSkillAliasGroups`' prompt (used by both 2.1a and this new check) to name the asymmetry explicitly — a missed match is harmless, a wrong match can put an unearned claim on the resume — after live testing separately found the dictionary re-making an already-fixed mistake (AWS merged back into EC2) on two different fresh accounts, plus a new one (generic "SQL" merged into the specific id "PostgreSQL") on a third. Verified live with the exact JD that first exposed the bug: `reactjs->react`, `nodejs->node.js`, and `github->git` were all correctly learned and closed, while the genuinely different "GitHub Actions" correctly stayed a separate, honest gap.

**Manual retries had no cap at all, despite section 5's documented "3, regardless of reason... shared across automatic and manual."** The `>= 3` check only ever lived inside `shouldRetryAutomatically()`, which governs the automatic sub-loop within one pass — neither manual retry-triggering route (`POST /:id/resume` with `action:'retry'`, and accepting a suggest-missing-skills bullet) had any cap check at all. Found live: an application already at `retryCount: 3` from its automatic retries climbed to `retryCount: 10` after 7 manual "send back with notes" calls, each a real, full LLM pass, with no error and no cap message at any point. Chosen fix: both routes now reject with a 400 once `retryCount` reaches the shared cap, checked before the graph is ever invoked, so a refused retry costs nothing — `approve` is deliberately unaffected regardless of `retryCount`.

**The tailored summary's "no first-person, no filler phrase" rule had no deterministic backstop, unlike the metric rule right above it in the same prompt.** Node 5's `SUMMARY_RULES` already explicitly bans "I"/"my"/"me" and named filler ("results-driven", "proven track record", etc.), but nothing ever checked whether the model actually complied — a real generation used "I am", "I have", and "proven track record" despite the rule being spelled out. Considered relying on further prompt tightening alone — rejected on the same evidence the metric rule's own history already established: a soft instruction, even an explicit one, isn't reliable enough for something this concretely checkable. Chosen: `summaryStyleCheck.js` scans every generated summary against the same "explicit hand-list, not a broad pattern" precedent already used for `SENIORITY_QUALIFIER_TERMS`; a violation triggers one targeted regeneration of just the summary (a smaller, separate call, not a full re-run of node 5's bullets), naming the exact violation found so the correction is aimed rather than another blind attempt. `TAILOR_PROMPT_VERSION` bumped so a previously-cached violating summary can't be silently reused. Verified live: a real application's automatic retry produced a summary containing "dynamic" (one of the banned terms), which the check caught and corrected before it reached the final output.

**The mandated "N+ years" phrasing almost always failed its own fabrication check.** Node 5 requires the summary state experience as "5+ years", never a raw decimal — but `verifySummary()`'s numeric allowlist only ever contained the plain figure and its floor/ceiling, never the "+"-suffixed form the model is required to use, so the mandated phrasing was extracted as its own token and flagged as a fabricated metric. Found live on two applications: one correctly failed verification for writing "5+ years" exactly as instructed (`unsupportedClaim`, "the claim of 5+ years of experience is unsupported"); a second only passed by coincidence, because an unrelated kept bullet happened to independently contain the literal substring "5+" ("5+ modules"). Because `unsupportedClaim` unconditionally vetoes the automatic-retry path regardless of any other flag, this false positive could silently disable the documented `missingRequirement` retry policy for almost any application. Chosen fix: add the floored figure's own "+" form to the allowlist. Verified live, including through the independent Re-check path, that the exact same phrasing now produces only genuine flags.

**`coverLetterGenerationNode` named the candidate's own current resume tagline as "the position," not the job being applied to.** It passed `state.resumeTitle` into the `<resume_title>` prompt slot — `atsScoreAndRecruiterNode`, one node later, already used the correct `state.tailoredTitle?.finalText || state.resumeTitle` expression for the identical purpose, but the cover-letter node was never updated to match. A real cover letter opened with "...the Fullstack & AI Engineer position..." while the actual job applied for was "Full Stack Product Engineer" — a candidate could unknowingly submit a letter naming the wrong role. Also found and fixed in the same pass: `generateCoverLetter.js`'s `computeInputHash` never included the title at all, so even after fixing the node, a title-only change (e.g. a human edit) wouldn't have invalidated a stale cached letter. Chosen fix: use the same resolved-title expression already correct one node later, add `resumeTitle` to the cache's input hash, and bump `COVER_LETTER_PROMPT_VERSION`. Verified live by reproducing the exact reported mismatch (resume title "Fullstack & AI Engineer" against a JD titled "Senior Full Stack Product Engineer") and confirming the letter now opens with the correct, resolved title.

**The skill-alias dictionary only ever grew from one place — the full resume-upload route — leaving every other place a genuinely new skill term gets typed invisible to it.** An in-app master-resume bullet add/edit, a hand-edited application bullet/summary, or an accepted skill-gap suggestion could all introduce a real new term (verified live: "Vercel," "test driven development"), but none of them ran the upload route's propose-groups-and-merge pipeline — so no matter how literally the word was written, every check that depends on the dictionary (Re-check, skill badges, skill frequency, suggest-missing-skills) stayed blind to it until a full resume re-upload happened to introduce the same term again. Chosen fix: extracted the upload route's inline "which of these terms are genuinely new" filter into a pure, testable `filterNewSkillTerms()`, then wrapped the existing propose-groups + add-entries pipeline behind one shared orchestrator, `growSkillDictionaryFromTerms()`, with its own internal try/catch so a growth failure can never block the save that triggered it — the same "never fails the primary action" guarantee section 2.1a's original upload-time version already had. Wired into all four previously-silent call sites, not just documented as a gap: master-resume bullet add/edit, hand-edited application bullet/summary text, and accepted skill-gap suggestions. Verified end-to-end against real account data rather than a synthetic fixture — a hand-edited bullet mentioning "Vercel"/"test driven development" now teaches the dictionary immediately, with no manual database fix required.

**The resume-upload skill-alias growth step (2.1a) had no guarantee that every new term it sent to the model actually made it into the dictionary.** `proposeSkillAliasGroups`' own prompt says "every input term must end up as an alias in exactly one group," but nothing ever checked that it did — same class of gap as the tailored-summary style rule and the mandated "N+ years" phrasing above, both of which needed a deterministic backstop because a soft instruction to an LLM isn't reliably followed. Found live: uploading an updated resume sent `["langgraph", "langchain", ...]` as new terms; the model correctly grouped "langchain" but silently omitted "langgraph" from every group in its response, with no error. Since `buildSkillMatchers` only ever recognizes terms that are actual keys/values in the dictionary, "langgraph" stayed permanently invisible to it — so a bullet and summary that both genuinely said "LangGraph" could never verify the "LangGraph" skill badge on the Master Resume page (`computeVerifiedSkills`), even though the word was really there. Chosen fix: `ensureAllTermsCovered()` (`skillAliasesStore.js`) runs after `mergeAliasEntries` with the same `newTerms` list that was sent to the model, and self-maps (`term -> term`) any term still missing from the dictionary afterward — the same safe, zero-fabrication-risk fallback pattern already used for force-including a skill-gap bullet. Wired into `addSkillAliasEntriesForUser`, which now takes `newTerms` as a third argument; the resume-upload route passes it through. Deliberately scoped to the upload-time path only — the JD-side alias-growth call (`matchUnresolvedSkillsToKnown`, used by node 3's retry) already guarantees full coverage by construction, since it's a closed-choice enum rather than free-form grouping, so there's nothing to backfill there. The existing user's dictionary was also patched directly so the already-uploaded resume shows correctly without needing a re-upload.

**What `NON_VERIFIABLE_CLAIM_IDS` is for, and how "unit-testing" ended up wrongly excluded by it.** The deterministic fabrication check (node 6, `verifyBullet`/`verifySummary`) works by comparing what a tailored bullet/summary *claims* against what the resume actually backs, flagging anything claimed-but-unbacked as fabricated. But `tagBullet.js`'s own prompt is deliberately told to *never* tag certain things as a skill in the first place — job titles ("Full Stack Engineer") and soft skills/activities ("mentoring", "code reviews", "architecture design", "UI responsiveness", "cross-functional collaboration"), since gap analysis and skill-matching only care about concrete technical skills. Side effect: if a tailored summary ever restates the candidate's own title or a soft skill, the fabrication check would treat it as a claim that can *structurally never* be backed (it was never eligible to be tagged as a resume skill to begin with) — a permanent false positive on something completely true, exactly what happened when a summary restating "Full Stack Engineer" got flagged (see the entry above this one in the log for that history). `NON_VERIFIABLE_CLAIM_IDS` is the fix for that: a short, explicit, hand-maintained list of canonical ids excluded *only* from the fabrication check (`extractVerifiableClaims`), never from the skill-badge display (`verifiedSkills.js` still sees everything, unfiltered) — so a resume can honestly restate its own title or a soft skill without a false alarm, while a real technical skill is still checked exactly as strictly as before. Deliberately a short explicit list rather than a broad pattern, precisely so a genuine hard skill could never be swept into it by accident — except one was: `'unit-testing'` had been added to this list too, even though it's a real, concrete technical practice that `tagBullet.js` tags completely normally (confirmed live: a bullet literally tagged with skill `"unit testing"`), not a soft skill or job title by any definition the list was built for. Found live via a real discrepancy: node 3's gap analysis (which never reads this list) correctly counted "unit-testing" as matched (4/8), while Re-check's live "still missing" list (`computeHumanRecheck` → `verifyBullet` → this exclusion) silently dropped it back out (3/8) for the exact same resume content, because `extractVerifiableClaims` filtered "unit-testing" out of `claimedSkills` before it ever reached `effectiveSkills`. Chosen fix: remove `'unit-testing'` from `NON_VERIFIABLE_CLAIM_IDS` — it never belonged there — with a test (`deterministicVerification.test.js`) asserting `verifyBullet` counts a genuine "unit testing" claim, reproducing the exact bullet that surfaced the bug.

**Re-uploading a resume this app itself exported surfaced two more real parsing bugs, unrelated to the heading-recognition one above — found by actually re-ingesting a real exported file, not by inspecting the parser's rules in isolation.** Both were caught the same way: running the deterministic parser directly against `Abdul_Raheem_Enigma_Resume.pdf` (a file downloaded from this app) and comparing its output to the source text.

**A wrapped bullet with no trailing punctuation at all was mistaken for a finished sentence, corrupting the next job's header.** `bulletTextLooksUnfinished()` (`segmentResume.js`) only checked for a trailing comma/colon/semicolon or an unmatched opening parenthesis — it had no rule for a bullet simply cut off mid-thought with nothing after it (e.g. "...using Next.js and OpenAI", wrapped right after "and"). Because the checker wrongly considered that bullet complete, its continuation line ("GPT-4, demonstrating proficiency in deploying applications on AWS.") got treated as the *next* job's header instead of being rejoined — corrupting that job's company field into a fragment of unrelated bullet text, which then poisoned the dedupe check the AI-recovery fallback (see the `dateLineCount` entry above) relies on, producing a fully duplicated resume. Chosen fix: a genuine, fully-extracted bullet essentially always ends in terminal punctuation (`.`/`!`/`?`); one that doesn't is now also treated as still-unfinished, alongside the existing comma/colon/paren checks. Verified against the real file directly (not just the new unit test): 38 duplicated bullets with a garbled header dropped back to the correct 19, with clean role/company fields.

**The "how many jobs should exist" date-counting check (see the `dateLineCount` entry above) counted every date-range line in the *entire* resume, including Education — inflating the count on any resume with more than one dated education entry and wrongly triggering the AI-recovery duplication even when every job had already parsed correctly.** Chosen fix: `countExperienceDateLines()` (`segmentResume.js`) reuses the exact same Experience-section boundary tracking `segmentResume()` itself already trusts, so it only counts date lines that actually fall inside the Experience section — an Education entry's own graduation date can no longer be mistaken for a possibly-missed job. `resumes.js` now calls this instead of scanning the raw text indiscriminately.

**The contact-info prefill parser split a contact line on the wrong separator character, and had no way to reassemble a URL wrapped across two lines — both found the same way, by re-uploading this app's own exported resume.** `extractContactInfo.js`'s location-building step only ever split a contact line on "•" (bullet), but this app's own export (`exportResumeDocx.js`) joins the contact line with "·" (middle dot) — a character the split never matched, so the whole line (location, phone, email, links all still stuck together, with their already-stripped phone/email leaving bare separators behind) fell through as one leftover chunk: `personalInfo.location` came out as literally `"Manchester, UK ·   ·   ·"`. Separately, a portfolio URL that PDF-wraps mid-hostname across two lines (`"https://abdulraheem-"` / `"rho.vercel.app"`) was truncated to just the first line's fragment, since the parser only ever looked at one line in isolation with no concept of a URL continuing onto the next. Chosen fix: split the contact line on either "•" or "·" (matching what the export actually writes, the same "sync writer and reader" fix as the WORK HISTORY entry above); and, when a URL sits right at the end of its line, peek at the next line — if it's a bare, whitespace-free fragment containing a "." or "/" (and not itself a heading/bullet/date line), glue it directly onto the URL with no separator, since a genuine unrelated line (a tagline, a new section) either contains spaces or matches one of those structural checks and is correctly left alone. Verified against the real exported file directly: location came back as a clean `"Manchester, UK"` and the portfolio field as the complete `"https://abdulraheem-rho.vercel.app"`.

**A stale, long-running server process meant every fix made earlier in this session had silently never taken effect until it was restarted.** The dev server had been started once, hours earlier, with the plain `node src/index.js` command (`npm start`, no file-watching) rather than `npm run dev` (`node --watch src/index.js`) — every code change made since then (the skill-dictionary fallback, the `NON_VERIFIABLE_CLAIM_IDS` fix, the `WORK HISTORY` revert, both `segmentResume.js` fixes) was sitting in the files but never actually running, since the process never reloads on its own. Found only because a fix that had just been verified working in isolation (via a standalone script run directly against the real file) still failed identically when re-tested through the actual running app. Fixed by killing the stale process and restarting it with `npm run dev` instead, so future edits take effect automatically. Worth remembering for any future session: a fix that works when tested directly against a service function, but doesn't show up in the running app, is a sign to check whether the server actually picked up the change — not a sign the fix itself is wrong.

**Node 3's JD-side canonical skills had the same "frozen snapshot" bug its resume side was fixed for earlier, just on the other half of the comparison.** `jdCanonicalSkills` self-canonicalized once, at first extraction, against whatever the dictionary looked like at that instant — if the dictionary later learned a different "official" spelling for the same phrase (e.g. a hand-edited bullet teaching "tailwind css" → `tailwind-css` after the JD had already self-canonicalized to the un-hyphenated form), the frozen value never found out, so a resume that genuinely covered the skill kept showing a false gap forever, on both a retry and a Re-check. Chosen fix: `deriveJdCanonicalSkills()` (`gapAnalysis.js`) re-derives the JD side from raw `jdKeywords` against the *current* dictionary on every call, mirroring exactly how the resume side already worked — used by both `computeGapAnalysis` (retries) and `computeHumanRecheck` (Re-check). This also let `gapAnalysisNode`'s JD-alias-learning pass drop a manual post-hoc substitution it used to layer on top, which was itself at risk of overwriting a correct live value with a stale one now that the underlying re-derivation already handles it. Verified against a real application: "tailwind css"/"radix ui"/"claude code" all correctly matched their hyphenated dictionary spelling afterward. A related client-only staleness bug was fixed alongside it: the Approval page's Skill Frequency panel only ever fetched once on mount, unlike the two panels next to it fed by the same `keywordGaps` — it now re-fetches whenever the application is re-saved or Re-check runs.

**Skill Frequency, the live keyword-gap count, and Re-check disagreed about what counts as "the resume," because each independently decided what to scan.** Node 3's original `keywordGaps` folded in Projects but not the tailored summary; Skill Frequency only ever scanned tailored bullets (no Projects, no summary); Re-check scanned bullets + summary but not Projects. Found live: a JD skill only ever mentioned in a Projects entry (e.g. "Next.js"/"Pinecone" in an old dissertation project, never in any work-experience bullet) counted as matched in the number shown on page load, while Skill Frequency honestly reported zero resume mentions for the same skill — and the "Skills Matched" count would silently get *worse* the moment Re-check ran, with nothing about the resume having actually changed. Chosen fix: extracted the deterministic half of the re-check computation into `computeCurrentCoverage()` (`applications.js`), now folding in `projectCanonicalSkills` the same way node 3 already does; `GET /:id` calls it directly and returns a live, non-persisted `currentKeywordGaps` field, so the number is correct from the very first page load instead of only self-correcting after a manual Re-check. Skill Frequency now also scans the tailored summary and Projects text, on the same scope. Node 3's own snapshot (`computeGapAnalysis`) is deliberately left untouched — it still serves the role-fit gate and the once-per-application JD-alias-learning pass, a legitimate, independent purpose that doesn't need to track live edits the way an on-screen count does.

**A JD requirement satisfied by the tailored text's own literal wording could still show as a permanent gap, because the dictionary — not the raw words on the page — is the only thing anything else in this pipeline ever checks against.** `tagBullet`'s LLM call can tag a sentence with a paraphrase (e.g. tagging "system design" as "System Architecture") that never becomes its own dictionary entry, so `extractClaimedSkills`, `keywordGaps`, and Skill Frequency — none of which have a text-scanning fallback — stay permanently blind to the literal phrase actually sitting in the exported resume. Considered a live, non-persisting scan for arbitrary text — rejected as too broad: a short, generic word could false-match an unrelated sentence with no JD relevance at all. Chosen instead: `findUnconfirmedLiteralSkillMatches()` (`skillAliasesStore.js`) is deliberately narrow — checked only against the current JD's own `keywordGaps`, using the same literal, word-boundary matcher (`buildLiteralMatcher`, shared with `skillFrequency.js`'s own no-dictionary-entry fallback) against the tailored text. A hit is surfaced to the human as "found, not yet confirmed" rather than auto-credited — matching this app's "you approve everything" principle rather than letting a literal-text coincidence self-certify a claim. **Add** (`POST /:id/skills/confirm`) self-maps the term into this user's dictionary permanently, the same safe pattern `ensureAllTermsCovered` already uses; **Ignore** (`POST /:id/skills/dismiss`) records it on the new `applications.dismissedSkills` field. That field is deliberately per-application, not per-user or dictionary-wide: a literal match being noise on one JD (an unrelated coincidental phrase) says nothing about whether the same skill is a genuine, deliberate claim on a different JD, so dismissing it must never leak across applications the way a real dictionary entry correctly does.

**The tailored summary's matched-skill mentions were left to the tailoring model's own free judgment about "what matters most," with nothing anchoring that judgment to the specific JD.** Found live: a summary opened with generic "React, TypeScript, and AWS" while skipping LangChain/LangGraph, even against a GenAI-focused posting — the prompt only said "name 2-3 of the matched skills that matter most," handing the model the entire matched-skills list with no signal for what "matters most" meant for *this* JD. Two deterministic ranking ideas were checked against the real data first and both failed: the JD's own stored title omitted the GenAI framing entirely (just "Full Stack Engineer"), and TypeScript/React were listed earlier than LangChain/LangGraph in the JD's own extracted skill order — so list-position or title alone couldn't recover the right answer. Since this is a genuine reading-comprehension judgment, not something recoverable from a cheap heuristic, it was isolated into its own small, closed-choice call (`rankSummarySkills.js`) — the same "known-real list, never invent one" pattern as `matchUnresolvedSkillsToKnown`'s JD-alias-learning: a `z.enum` schema built from the already-confirmed `matchedSkills`, so the model can only select/order from a real list. The first prompt draft still under-weighted LangGraph (picked LangChain but not LangGraph); rereading the actual JD text showed why — a flat requirements checklist gives every category equal visual weight, while the posting's title/overview/role description repeated "GenAI" as its actual theme. The prompt was strengthened to weigh that repeated framing over checklist position, and re-verified against the real application: it now correctly produces LangChain+LangGraph ahead of TypeScript+React. `tailorContent.js`'s summary rule now names 3-4 skills (was 2-3), drawn only from this pre-ranked `summarySkills` input rather than freely chosen from the full matched-skills list, which stays untouched and complete for fabrication-checking elsewhere. Bumped to `tailor-v13`.

**Server-side pagination replaced "fetch the whole application history and filter client-side," once that history was large enough for the difference to matter.** `GET /api/applications` now takes `page`/`pageSize`/`search`/`status`/`ats`/`coverLetter`/`resume`/`sortKey`/`sortDir` and returns one filtered+sorted page via a MongoDB aggregation, backed by new `{userId, updatedAt}`/`{userId, status}` indexes. Chosen because client-side filtering only scales as long as the whole list is small enough to ship on every load — a design that was fine at the "just me" scale this app started at, but not indefinitely. A `hasApprovedApplications` flag is now returned alongside the page explicitly, since it used to be derived client-side from the full list, which pagination no longer hands over. Bundled in the same pass: a "Clear" link on the resume-upload dialog, added because the dialog's form state lives in the page component (which never unmounts), so a previously-closed-without-submitting dialog's pre-filled fields would otherwise persist stale into the next open.

**An approved application that never requested a cover letter at submission time had no way to get one later, short of a full new application.** Chosen fix: `POST /:id/generate-cover-letter`, scoped to `status === 'approved'` only, reuses the existing `generateCoverLetter()` service and its `GenerationCache` unchanged — no new generation logic — reading `matchedSkills` (not persisted on the `applications` document itself) fresh from the still-live LangGraph checkpoint, the same way `computeHumanRecheck` already does for post-approval reads. Safe specifically because approval already locks bullet/summary/title editing, so every input this call needs is guaranteed stable — this route would not be safe to expose on a `pending_approval` application, where those inputs are still moving. Verified end-to-end against a real approved application: correct, application-specific text generated and a real `GenerationCache` row created. A separate, unrelated fix landed in the same pass: the server's dev script moved from `node --watch` to `nodemon`, after the built-in watcher silently failed to pick up a file change mid-session with no indication anything was wrong — the exact failure mode the "stale server process" entry above already covers, now with a tool swap that doesn't reproduce it.

**The exported `.docx`/PDF layout was benchmarked directly against a resume built with a real resume-building tool (TealHQ), rather than iterated on by eye.** Comparing side by side surfaced several concrete gaps: unset page margins (defaulting to docx's generic 1in) meant identical content spilled onto a third page — fixed to explicit 0.5in margins, dropping it back to two. Every entry-level heading (job/degree/project/certification/volunteer) was colored and enlarged like a section label, reading far "heavier" than a real template — changed to bold-but-plain-black, reserving color for true top-level section labels (SUMMARY/EXPERIENCE/...), then given its own explicit size (1pt above body text, still smaller than a section heading) so it sits visibly between the two. A job's date was stacked on its own italic line below the header instead of right-aligned on the same line via a tab stop — the tab position itself had been computed against an assumed Letter-width page (12240 twips) while the document actually renders at A4 (11906 twips, matching the benchmark resume), a 334-twip gap that pushed every date past the true right margin; page size is now declared explicitly and the tab position derived from it so the two can't drift apart again. Every heading level now declares its own font explicitly rather than relying on `docDefaults`, since LibreOffice doesn't reliably inherit a document-wide font for named paragraph styles without a fontScheme/theme part — headings were silently falling back to Liberation Sans while body text correctly rendered in Carlito. Email/LinkedIn/portfolio became real clickable hyperlinks in the `.docx` (verified against the file's own relationship data), styled as plain unstyled text per request; LinkedIn's displayed text now carries its own `https://` prefix to match portfolio's already-prefixed form, since a bare `linkedin.com/...` wasn't reliably auto-detected as a link the way an already-prefixed URL is in the PDF viewer — the PDF export itself has no real embedded hyperlink annotations at all, a confirmed LibreOffice headless conversion limitation reproduced even with a minimal single-link test file. The identical margin and font-declaration fixes were applied to the cover letter export, which had the same gap.

**Two more real-resume parsing/layout bugs surfaced from the same benchmarking pass, on top of the export fixes above.** A genuine 3-part job header (role alone on its own line, then "Company • Location" sharing the date's line) could have its own-line split coincidentally match a job-title keyword *inside* the company name (e.g. "Developer" inside "Independent Developer"), wrongly discarding the real buffered role — fixed, and the 3 already-corrupted `resumeBullets` documents patched directly, since a parsing fix alone doesn't retroactively repair existing data. Separately, Experience entries were exporting as one run-on "Company — Role" line — changed to Role on top (larger, with the date) and Company on its own line below (smaller), consistent with the entry-heading sizing fix above.

**Outreach Email Generation — a per-contact AI-drafted outreach email, extending the Outreach Tracker feature — was built as a deliberately lighter-weight sibling to the resume-tailoring pipeline, not a scaled-down copy of it.** Brainstormed in a design doc, then implemented via a 6-task plan under subagent-driven development (one implementer + one task reviewer per task, plus a final whole-branch review). Several choices intentionally diverge from how the JD pipeline handles its own LLM calls:

- **No `GenerationCache`.** Every other LLM-calling node in this app (nodes 1, 5, 7, 9) caches by `(applicationId, nodeName, inputHash, promptVersion, model)` because that pipeline retries and checkpoints. Outreach email generation is a single manual action the user triggers once per contact and reviews immediately — there is nothing to retry and no checkpoint to resume, so a cache would add bookkeeping with nothing to protect. `server/src/services/generateOutreachEmail.js` is a thin wrapper with no cache lookup at all, same precedent as `generateCoverLetter.js` having no dedicated test for the same "can't assert on a real model call" reason.
- **`OutreachCompany.masterResumeId` is a plain Mongoose `String`, not an `ObjectId`/`ref`.** The field's "no resume linked" state is an empty string; casting `''` to `ObjectId` throws. Making it a plain string sidesteps the CastError entirely and keeps `validateOutreachCompany.js` free of any Mongoose import, so it stays unit-testable with plain `node:test` like every other pure validator in this codebase. Ownership is checked manually (`MasterResume.findOne({_id, userId})`) only at the moment a generation actually needs it, and resolving to nothing (empty, malformed, or not owned) is a graceful no-resume-context fallback, never a thrown error.
- **Generation is sequential, not `Promise.all`, across a multi-contact batch.** A deliberate choice so one contact's failure doesn't abort the rest of the batch, and so several LLM calls aren't bursted at once for what's meant to be a manual, reviewed-as-you-go action. Each contact's result (success or error) lands independently in the `drafts` array.
- **The contact `category` keyword list is duplicated, not shared, between `client/src/lib/guessContactCategory.js` and `server/src/services/validateOutreachCompany.js`'s `CONTACT_CATEGORY_ENUM`.** No shared package exists between `client/` and `server/` in this repo (two independent `package.json`s) — duplicating a 4-item list by hand was judged simpler than introducing shared-package tooling for it. The category a contact ends up with on save still goes through the server's own enum validation regardless of what the client guessed; the two are kept in sync by hand, confirmed identical (including the "Talent & HR" ampersand) at every review pass.

**A whole-branch review after all 6 tasks caught three real bugs that six individual task reviews couldn't — because all three traced back to the plan's own code snippets, which every task review correctly checked itself against.** (1) Every company save re-creates its `contacts` array server-side, so every contact gets a brand-new Mongo `_id` on every save — harmless while `_id` was only a React list key, but Tasks 5-6 promoted it to part of the Contacts tab's selection identity (`${companyId}:${contactId}`) without making it stable. Selecting contacts, then saving an edit to either contact's company, silently emptied the next "Generate emails" click's target list — the dialog closed with zero drafts and no error shown. Fixed with an effect that prunes stale selection keys whenever the company list changes, plus a guard that surfaces a visible error instead of silently closing when nothing resolves to generate. (2) The resume-link dropdown labeled every option with the candidate's own name (`personalInfo.fullName`, identical across all of a user's resumes) instead of `label` (the field that actually distinguishes "Full-stack CV" from "AI Engineering CV") — unusable with more than one resume. Fixed by swapping the fallback order. (3) The Contacts tab silently inherited the Companies tab's search/filters (since `allContacts` derives from the same already-filtered `companies` list) with no visible indication and no way to clear it from that tab — fixed with a one-line hint shown only when a filter is active, reusing the existing `clearFilters()` rather than duplicating it. All three are now recorded as a durable reminder that a plan's own example code is still code, and still needs the same scrutiny the rest of the diff gets.

**The Outreach UI was rebuilt as list → per-company detail page, replacing the cross-company Contacts tab the plan had originally specified.** The first build put contacts in their own top-level tab alongside Companies, flattened across every company — which is what produced the inherited-filter bug above, and more fundamentally meant the two things a user actually does together (read a company's notes, draft an email to someone there) lived on different screens. Chosen instead to follow the pattern Master Resumes already uses: a plain list, a row click into a detail page, and everything about one company on that page — company fields on the left as a display ↔ edit toggle, and on the right the resume selector, contact checklist, generate bar, and saved drafts. Contacts are editable only inside the left side's Edit mode, so there's exactly one place a contact can be changed, rather than two that have to agree. Deleting stayed on the list row (where you'd look to delete a whole company) while editing moved to the detail page, since the list's inline edit dialog is what the detail page replaces.

**The outreach email prompt was fixed against real generated output across five rounds, not by reading the prompt and reasoning about it — the same lesson the tailored summary's metric rule already taught, re-learned on a new surface.** Each round came from reading an actual generated email and finding one concrete thing wrong, and each fix is pinned by a substring test in `outreachEmailPrompt.test.js` (now 25 tests) so a later prompt edit can't silently undo it:

- **Length and structure were my guess, and the guess was wrong.** Earlier edits had drifted toward bulleted lists and multiple paragraphs, which read as "off" without it being obvious why. Replaced with a figure from actual cold-email research (a Hubspot analysis of 40 million emails: 50–125 words maximizes reply rate) — the prompt now targets 80–130 words, at most two short paragraphs, no bulleted list.
- **The company notes never reached the model at all.** The one field carrying everything specific about a company was simply not threaded into the prompt, so every "why this company" sentence was generic by construction. Added as its own `<company_notes>` tag, omitted entirely when empty rather than sent as a blank tag.
- **A cited project got paraphrased instead of named** ("a legal document assistant" for a project with a real name given right there in the context). The prompt now requires the exact given name verbatim, with an explicit allowance to cite just the core name before a descriptive dash-suffix, and a ban on casual meta-labeling ("this is my recent work", "I completed a project on X") in place of weaving it into a sentence.
- **The greeting ran into the body** ("Lewis, reaching out to inquire…"). Now required on its own line, followed by a blank line.
- **A no-resume draft signed off with a literal `[Your Name]`.** The instruction had said to leave the sign-off bare; the model filled the gap with a placeholder anyway. Now bans the placeholder explicitly, by example.
- **The hardest one: a professional opening undercut by academic framing** — "As a full-stack engineer with over 5 years of experience, I recently completed a legal AI dissertation project…". The tempting fix was to strip the offending wording from one field, but tracing it showed *two* independent sources carried it (the project's own stored name, which ends in "- MSc Dissertation Project", and the resume summary text, which says "dissertation" outright). Fixing either one alone would have left the other to reproduce it. Chosen to fix at the instruction level instead: keep the framing internally consistent, and describe what was built or its outcome rather than its academic category, even when the candidate background itself uses that language.

**The prompt's illustrative examples had the developer's own project name and degree baked into them — a generic prompt that quietly wasn't generic.** Found by the user reading the prompt rather than its output: the shared system instructions cited "SolicitorSense AI" and "MSc project" as the examples of how to name a project, which every user's every email would have been steered by. The distinction that made this a real bug rather than a style nit: the *dynamic* per-call inputs (resume, notes, contact) were always correctly per-user — it was only the static example text inside the shared instruction block that was personal. Replaced with a fictional "Project Nova", with the real test being whether an example could identify any specific person.

**A generated draft is persisted on the contact and survives an unrelated company edit.** `normalizeOutreachCompanyPayload()` rebuilds every contact field-by-field on save specifically so stray client fields can't reach the database — which also meant it silently dropped `lastGeneratedEmail` every time the company was edited, wiping drafts as a side effect of changing, say, the location. Chosen to pass the field through explicitly (a conditional spread, so a contact with no draft still gets no empty object) rather than loosen the rebuild, keeping the "nothing extra gets persisted" guarantee intact while naming the one field that is legitimately server-owned state. The client's `editFormFromCompany()` mapping needed the same field added for the same reason.

**Four UI bugs, all fixed only after reproducing them in a real browser — and the first fix attempt failed precisely because it was verified by computed geometry instead.** The "which role" input visibly overlapped the Goal dropdown, yet every `getBoundingClientRect()` check on the named wrapper elements came back correct, so the first fix (flex-wrap plus min-widths) was reasonably believed to work and did not. The user's screenshot is what disproved it. Re-checking with `document.elementsFromPoint()` at the actual overlap found the cause: shadcn's `SelectTrigger` carries `w-fit` in its own base classes, so the *button* rendered at 306px (sized to its content) inside a correctly-sized 224px wrapper — a parent's width is simply not what it reads. Every other `Select` in the app already passed `className="w-full"`; four new call sites hadn't. The other three: the contacts column's `max-w-xs truncate` did nothing because a default `table-layout: auto` sizes columns by content before any per-cell `max-width` applies (fixed with `table-fixed` plus explicit column widths); the Add-company dialog had been reduced to two fields and was restored to the full form; and a phantom horizontal scrollbar with no oversized element anywhere turned out to be CSS-spec behavior — setting `overflow-y: auto` auto-promotes the other axis from `visible` to `auto` — fixed with an explicit `overflow-x-hidden` on `<main>`, verified not to regress the Applications page's own internally-scrolling table. The durable lesson, worth more than any of the four: for a layout bug, a passing geometry assertion on the element you suspected is not evidence — take the screenshot and hit-test the actual pixel.

**The Notes field got an info tooltip rather than a placeholder or a rename, because its real job isn't obvious from its name.** Notes is the only input the email generator has for anything company-specific — there's no website fetching (see "Paste JD text directly; no URL fetching" above, which this feature deliberately follows rather than carving an exception out of), so a vague note produces a vague "why this company" sentence and there is no other source to fall back on. Reused the existing `InfoTooltip` from the Approval page by importing it across folders, rather than relocating it to a shared directory — relocating would have touched its six existing unrelated call sites for no behavioral gain.
