# Key Decisions Log

A running record of the architectural choices behind the Job Application Agent and why they were made — for quick reference, so the reasoning doesn't have to be re-derived from scratch in a future session. Each entry names what we chose and, briefly, what it was chosen over.

**Retrieval, not generation, for tailoring.** Resume content lives as individual `resumeBullets` records; the tailoring node selects and rephrases from them rather than writing from a blank page. Chosen over free-form generation because it removes most of the surface area for fabrication structurally, rather than relying on the model to police itself.

**Deterministic verification as its own node, before scoring.** A dedicated node checks every tailored claim against its source bullet, separate from and running before ATS scoring. Chosen because "is this true" and "does this score well" are different questions — folding them together lets a resume that reads well but overstates something slip through unnoticed.

**Retry policy driven by named flags, not a raw ATS score.** The loop only retries on specific problems (missing requirement, unsupported claim, poor readability); a low score alone doesn't trigger a retry. Chosen over threshold-based retry because optimizing straight at an LLM-estimated score rewards keyword stuffing rather than genuine improvement.

**Role fit gate before tailoring.** A cheap overlap check (plus an LLM call only for ambiguous cases) exits early when a JD and resume are a poor match — e.g. a recruiter JD against a software-engineer resume — before any tailoring or scoring spend. Chosen because without it, a bad-fit JD would still burn three full retry cycles for no benefit, since there's no more relevant material to surface on attempt three than attempt one.

**The role fit gate's low-overlap floor was removed — a numeric overlap percentage can't structurally distinguish wrong-stack from wrong-discipline.** Node 4 originally auto-rejected (no LLM call at all) whenever canonical skill overlap fell below 20%, in addition to the ≥50% free fast-pass. Chosen after finding, via live testing against five real postings against an actual uploaded resume, that a same-discipline JD with a different tech stack produces the identical signal as a genuinely cross-discipline one: a Java/Spring backend JD scored a flat 0% overlap against a Node.js/React resume and was auto-rejected, and a Node.js/React/Python JD — literally naming that resume's own top two skills — scored 19%, one point under the floor, and was auto-rejected without ever reaching the stage-2 check that already judges this distinction correctly. No floor value fixes this, because it isn't a mistuned number — a low-but-same-discipline overlap and a genuinely cross-discipline overlap are indistinguishable from the raw percentage alone, so there's no floor value strictly between "still makes this mistake" and "removed entirely." A denominator reweight (e.g. top-N skills, core-vs-nice-to-have weighting) doesn't fix it either, since it doesn't change that the compared skill tokens are simply different words — closing that gap would mean hand-building a discipline taxonomy that duplicates, with less confidence, exactly what the stage-2 prompt already does correctly. Chosen to remove the floor entirely rather than retune it: only the ≥50% fast-*pass* remains free, since that direction is safe — high overlap can never itself indicate a discipline mismatch — while everything else, including 0% or unmeasurable overlap, now defers to the stage-2 LLM call. Accepted trade-off: most real submissions that aren't a slam-dunk match now cost one extra cheap gpt-4o-mini call instead of being free when they're a bad fit.

**Human approval as the final gate, with AI/human/final text kept distinct.** Nothing is saved without explicit review, and the state model tracks what the model generated, what was hand-edited, and what was actually approved as three separate fields. Chosen so it's always possible to tell what came from the AI and what came from me, rather than losing that distinction the moment an edit is made.

**Resume parsing lives outside the per-JD pipeline entirely.** Uploading and parsing a resume into bullets is a one-time operation; a LangGraph thread only ever reads already-parsed bullets, never parses a file itself. Chosen over parsing-per-run because parsing is expensive and unnecessary to repeat, and because it's what made clean support for multiple resumes possible.

**Multiple master resumes, capped at 5, with a full cascade on delete.** Deleting a resume removes every linked application, generated file, and thread history before the resume itself is removed. Chosen to keep the resume list manageable and to avoid orphaned data referencing a resume that no longer exists.

**In-app bullet editing is a direct, in-place update.** Editing a bullet changes that database record directly, with no versioning or history kept. Chosen for simplicity over an additive/versioned approach (keep the old bullet, add a new one, mark the old deprecated) — the tradeoff, made knowingly: an old application's `sourceBulletId` can end up pointing at wording that's since changed, so it can't be used as a perfect historical record of what a past application actually claimed.

**No batching, no queue — one JD processed at a time.** Batch submission and BullMQ-based parallel processing were designed, then deliberately removed. Chosen because this is a single-person tool, not a multi-tenant service, and the simplicity was worth more than the throughput.

**Paste JD text directly; no URL fetching.** The pipeline never fetches a job posting server-side — the JD is pasted in, along with a company name and an optional, never-fetched reference link for personal recall later. Chosen over URL scraping because pasted text is more accurate (no JS-rendering gaps, no login walls, no scraped nav/footer noise) and removes an entire category of risk — SSRF exposure and job-board ToS issues — that scraping would have introduced.

**Skill matching runs on a canonicalized taxonomy, not raw text.** JD and resume skill terms are normalized against a controlled alias dictionary (e.g. "Node.js" / "Node" / "NodeJS" → one canonical ID) before any gap analysis. Chosen because a raw set difference produces false gaps from surface-form mismatches alone, which would otherwise tempt the tailoring step to "fill" gaps that were never really there.

**Every node is idempotent; every generation is versioned.** LLM results are cached by `(applicationId, nodeName, inputHash, promptVersion, model)`, and every generation records the resume version, prompt version, and model used. Chosen because the combination of retries, checkpointing, and LLM calls makes accidental double-execution likely without it, and because prompt/model changes are otherwise impossible to distinguish from resume changes months later.

**Re-checking a hand-edited resume stores its own score, separate from the AI's.** Editing the tailored text yourself doesn't automatically re-score it — a non-blocking "re-check" action does that on request, and the result (`humanRecheckAtsScore`/`Flags`) is kept apart from the AI-generated `atsScore`/`atsFlags` rather than overwriting them. Chosen for the same reason `generatedText` and `humanEditedText` are kept separate elsewhere: losing which score belongs to which version of the text would make the record less trustworthy, not more convenient.

**Skill suggestions during approval add a real resume bullet — never insert text directly into the tailored output.** When a JD wants a skill you have real experience with but never added to your resume, accepting the suggestion routes through the same one-time bullet-creation flow used for uploads (write the bullet, tag it, save it to `resumeBullets`), then re-tailors from there. Chosen over direct insertion because that shortcut would recreate the exact fabrication risk every other guardrail in this pipeline exists to prevent — content with no `sourceBulletId`, unverifiable, and indistinguishable from something the model invented.

**Export generates files on demand, not as an automatic graph side effect.** The original node 11 design wrote `.docx`/`tracker.xlsx` automatically once a run reached a terminal state. In practice this was replaced with `GET` routes that build the file fresh from MongoDB whenever it's requested, gated on `status === 'approved'`, and never persist it server-side. Chosen because on-demand generation is simpler to keep correct — there's no separate "exported" state to fall out of sync with the underlying data, and re-downloading after a later edit just reflects whatever is current in Mongo rather than a stale file from whenever node 11 last ran.

**A retry re-derives the resume's skill list from current bullets, not a snapshot from the first pass.** Both the manual and suggest-missing-skills retry paths route back through gap analysis before re-tailoring, recomputing `resumeCanonicalSkills`/`keywordGaps` from whatever `resumeBullets` currently holds. Chosen after finding that a bullet added mid-flow (e.g. to plug a JD gap) had no effect on the displayed gap list, since gap analysis previously only ever ran once, on the very first pass — silently stale from that point on regardless of what changed afterward.

**A hand-edited bullet or summary survives a retry instead of being overwritten by the fresh regeneration.** Retrying re-runs the tailoring model, which naturally produces entirely new text for every bullet. Anything with `editSource: "human"` from the previous round is now carried forward as-is rather than replaced. Chosen because silently discarding a review edit the moment another retry happens would violate the same "AI/human/final text kept distinct" guarantee this pipeline already promises elsewhere — an edit made to fix a real problem (e.g. a fabricated-skill flag) has no business disappearing just because an unrelated retry ran afterward.

**The summary half of that guarantee only actually worked for bullets, not summaries — fixed by re-syncing `tailoredSummary` into the graph on retry, the same way `tailoredBullets` already was.** `PATCH /:id/summary` writes a hand-edit straight to the Mongo `applications` document, but never touched the LangGraph checkpoint's own `state.tailoredSummary`. `tailorContentNode`'s preservation check (`state.tailoredSummary?.editSource === 'human' ? state.tailoredSummary : generated.tailoredSummary`) was correctly written, but every retry path (`POST /:id/resume`, `POST /:id/suggest-skills/accept`) only ever re-synced `tailoredBullets` from Mongo into the resume payload — never `tailoredSummary` — so the graph's own state never learned a human had touched it, and the check always saw stale pre-edit state. Found via live testing: hand-editing a summary, then accepting a suggest-missing-skills bullet (an unrelated, legitimate retry trigger), silently reverted the summary to a fresh AI generation with no error. Chosen to fix by including `tailoredSummary: application.tailoredSummary` in both resume-Command payloads and having `humanApprovalNode`'s retry branch pass it through (`resumeValue.tailoredSummary ?? state.tailoredSummary`) — mirroring exactly what already worked for `tailoredBullets`, rather than inventing a new mechanism.

**A bullet added to plug a JD skill gap is force-included verbatim, not left to the model's discretion.** Accepting a suggest-missing-skills bullet only ever nudged the tailoring model with a soft note ("consider incorporating this if relevant") — the model remained free to drop it, and did. Chosen to instead deterministically append that specific bullet's original text if the model's own selection doesn't include it, since the entire reason it exists is to close that gap; leaving it to chance defeats the point of adding it. Verbatim inclusion (no extra LLM call) also means zero additional fabrication risk.

**Bullet selection guarantees at least one bullet per real employer.** Node 5 originally selected "the 3-6 most relevant bullets" from the whole resume as one flat pool, with no rule protecting employer coverage — which let an entire job disappear from a tailored resume if none of its bullets ranked among the top few for a given JD. Chosen to deterministically add back the highest-JD-overlap bullet for any employer left unrepresented, because an unexplained employment gap reads far worse on a real resume than one moderately-relevant bullet would.

**Node 5 keeps every candidate bullet by default and only excludes one judged genuinely out of context, instead of selecting a top-N subset.** The tailoring model previously picked "around 15-16 relevant bullets" out of the full candidate pool — anything not picked simply vanished, with no record it was ever considered. Chosen to flip the default: the model now returns a `rejected` verdict for every single candidate bullet, defaulting to keep, and reserves `rejected: true` for a bullet that's genuinely irrelevant to the JD's domain, not merely weaker than another candidate. Rejected bullets still flow into `tailoredBullets` (verbatim, `rephraseIntensity: 0`) so the human reviewer can see exactly what got excluded and why — tagged, read-only, on the Approval page — while every downstream node (cover letter, style lint, ATS scoring, the verification summary check) filters them out before use, and the `.docx` export filters them out before the file is built. This also meant the two existing safety nets (force-included skill-gap bullet, guaranteed employer coverage) had to change from "append if missing" to "un-reject in place if present but rejected," since the candidate pool no longer omits anything.

**The skills list is editable in two independent scopes, and stays purely cosmetic either way.** `masterResumes.skills` can be edited permanently (the master-resume edit dialog); `applications.tailoredSkills` is an optional per-application override of that same list, scoped to just one export, falling back to the live master list when unset — the same "override with fallback" shape as `tailoredBullets`/`tailoredSummary`. Neither ever feeds gap analysis, ATS scoring, or fabrication verification — confirmed by tracing every consumer of `skills`. Chosen because letting an edit here move the score or close a keyword gap would be the exact shortcut every other guardrail in this pipeline exists to prevent: typing a word into a list and getting uncontested credit for it. Each chip is additionally color-coded — solid if the skill is genuinely found in real bullet text (`computeVerifiedSkills`, reusing the same text-scanning matcher already trusted for fabrication checks), outline if it's merely self-declared — but this signal is display-only too, never fed back into anything upstream.

**Resume-upload contact fields are deterministically pre-filled from the file, but never auto-saved.** Selecting a file now runs a rule-based (no LLM) scan of the document's first few lines — name, phone, email, LinkedIn/portfolio URLs, a title tagline — and pre-populates the upload form's fields, plus a suggested label. Chosen over leaving every field blank because most of this is copy-able straight off the page with simple pattern matching, and chosen over an LLM call because it's cheap, deterministic, and this is a one-time upload-time convenience, not something worth spending a model call on. Every field stays a normal editable input and nothing is written to `masterResumes` until the user reviews and submits — this only changes what the blank form starts with, never what "you approve everything" means.

**Deterministic section-parsing gained two layout tolerances it didn't originally have.** Uploading a real resume (the developer's own) surfaced two bugs: a resume with no explicit `SUMMARY`-style heading lost its summary paragraph entirely, and a resume with "degree name on its own line, institution+location+date together on the next line" (rather than "degree+date share a line, institution follows") had its degree and institution fields silently swapped and shifted between entries. Fixed by adding an implied-summary fallback (preamble prose left over once name/contact-line/tagline-line patterns are excluded, used only when no explicit heading is ever found) and a second education-parsing branch keyed on which line the date actually shares. Chosen over requiring a specific resume template, since the whole point of file upload is accepting a resume as it already exists, not making the user reformat it to satisfy the parser.

**The tailored summary never states an achievement metric at all — only the years-of-experience figure.** Two earlier, softer rules were tried and both failed empirically against a live retry: "include exactly one metric" and "don't restate a number that's already in a bullet" both still produced a summary repeating two or three numbers verbatim from the bullets shown right below it. The realization: in this retrieval-only pipeline, every metric available to the summary necessarily also lives in a bullet, since bullets are the *only* place a metric can come from — so a conditional "unless it's already in a bullet" rule is nearly always vacuously true, and asking the model to self-verify that condition during generation is exactly the kind of soft instruction it doesn't reliably comply with. Replacing the conditional with a flat prohibition (no metric at all, ever, except years-of-experience) is what actually stopped the repetition when tested live — confirmed by retrying the same real application against each prompt version and reading the result back from the generation cache, not by inspecting the prompt text alone.

**The skill-alias dictionary is now grown by AI at resume-upload time, instead of hand-written.** `server/data/skillAliases.json` started as a fixed, manually-maintained list of synonyms ("Node"/"NodeJS"/"Node.js" → one canonical id). That doesn't scale past the one stack it was written against — uploading a resume for a different domain entirely (tested live with a security/door-supervisor CV) meant every one of its skill terms was invisible to gap analysis and to the deterministic fabrication check, since neither can recognize a term with zero entries. Considered generating aliases fresh on every use instead of persisting them — rejected, because it would mean re-deriving the same fixed fact (e.g. "Node" = "Node.js") on every single run, at real cost, with a real chance an LLM call answers slightly differently between runs, undermining the exact determinism the fabrication check depends on. Chosen instead: after a resume introduces genuinely new terms, one batched call proposes canonical groupings, merged in with a hard safety rule — never overwrite an alias that already points elsewhere, and redirect a proposed canonical id that's itself an existing alias to its real target, so two competing "true names" for one concept can never coexist. Computed once, at upload (already a one-time cost per resume), then reused for free on every future gap-analysis/verification call — not re-derived per JD.

**`canonicalizeSkill.js`/`deterministicVerification.js` read the alias dictionary through one shared, reloadable store, not their own independent copies.** Both used to `readFileSync` the same file at their own module load — meaning a write during upload (see above) would never be seen by the already-running server process without a restart. Chosen to centralize loading behind `skillAliasesStore.js`, with an explicit reload called right after every write, so a newly-learned alias is visible to the very next request. The same store also had to stop crashing the server outright when the dictionary file doesn't exist yet (a fresh clone, or the very first-ever upload with no file at all) — it now starts with an empty dictionary and the file is created on first write, rather than throwing at startup.

**"AWS" and "EC2" are separate canonical skills, not one merged id.** They used to share one canonical id, which meant a resume that only ever said "AWS" got credit for an "Amazon EC2" skill badge with zero evidence EC2 specifically was ever used. EC2 is one specific AWS service (virtual servers), not the whole platform — treating them as identical was a pre-existing, un-scrutinized dictionary entry, not a new decision, until this narrowing found it.

**The verified-skill badge distinguishes an exact wording match from a same-meaning-different-wording match.** Previously a skill either showed as backed by your bullets or not, with no way to tell "the literal word is there" from "only a synonym of it is there" (e.g. a "Node.js" badge backed by bullet text that only ever says "Node"). Worth surfacing because this app's own matching is deliberately synonym-aware (see above), but some real-world ATS software at actual employers is not — it does literal keyword scanning. Chosen to add a second, "partial" match state (checked by testing the skill's own literal text against the source, separately from the existing alias-based check) so the difference is visible without changing what counts as verified at all, and without touching gap analysis or scoring, which stay synonym-aware on purpose (see "don't optimize toward the wrong number").

**The deterministic anti-fabrication check excludes a fixed, short list of soft-skill/job-title canonical ids from what counts as a checkable "claim."** Teaching the alias dictionary to recognize terms like "Mentorship," "Code Review," and "Full-Stack Engineer" (so the verified-skill badge could credit them) had a side effect: the same recognition feeds the fabrication check, which compares "skills a bullet claims" against skills that were actually part of JD-matching — a vocabulary that deliberately never includes job titles or soft skills in the first place (`tagBullet.js`'s own prompt excludes them). Recognizing one of these terms as a claim therefore made it permanently unprovable, and a real application's tailored summary restating its own job title ("...as a Full Stack Engineer...") was flagged as a fabricated skill. Considered filtering these terms out of the badge's recognition instead — rejected, since that would just resurrect the original bug the recognition was added to fix. Chosen instead: a short, explicit, hand-maintained exclusion list used only by the fabrication check, never by the badge — so a term stays recognized for display purposes but is never treated as something requiring proof. Deliberately not a broad pattern-based rule, so a genuine hard skill can never be swept into the exclusion by accident; backed by a test asserting a real fabricated hard skill is still caught exactly as before.

**Resume parsing gained an AI-based fallback, used only when the deterministic pass looks broken, rather than another hand-written rule per new layout.** Testing against two resumes with layouts genuinely different from the one this parser was originally tuned against (a security/hospitality CV, a Flutter developer CV) surfaced six separate failures at once: bullet glyphs that don't survive PDF text extraction (producing zero bullets — a resume with none can't even be used to create an application), a date range losing its connecting dash to a multi-column layout, section headings only recognized in ALL CAPS, a bare location line bleeding into the summary, a job's location mistaken for its role/company when it shared a line with the date, and a job written with no bullet points at all silently vanishing even while sitting next to properly-bulleted ones. The cheap, safe fixes (a looser date regex, Title-Case headings, recognizing a location line, preferring an already-complete header buffered above a location that shares the date line) were applied directly. For the rest — genuinely no bullet marker left in the text at all, or a section's content scattered by column ordering — no new regex could safely generalize without risking a false match on some other, correctly-formatted resume. Chosen instead: keep the free, deterministic pass as the default for every resume (near-zero cost, and it's already correct most of the time), and only when it comes back looking clearly broken — zero bullets, an empty or visibly wrong education/skills result, an oversized summary, or fewer distinct jobs found than date-range lines in the text — ask an AI to find the same boundaries, copying bullet/section text out verbatim rather than generating anything. No fabrication risk either way: the AI is only ever asked where the boundaries are, never to invent content, since the actual text still comes from the document either way. Found live that the job-recovery fallback was itself non-deterministic on a first pass (it silently dropped a lightly-described job under a secondary "Other Experience" heading roughly half the time) — fixed by making the prompt state explicitly that a brief, secondary, or non-bulleted job counts exactly as much as a fully-bulleted one, then confirmed 5/5 repeat calls before trusting it.

**Every `resumeBullets` document now stores its own position, and every route that lists them sorts by it.** Bullets never had a stored order at all — display order depended entirely on MongoDB's own read order for a collection, which is not a guaranteed match for insertion order. Found live: two uploads of the identical PDF came back with the same bullets in a different sequence. Chosen to add an explicit `order` field, populated at every creation site (initial upload, in that document's own order; a manually added bullet, or one added via a suggest-missing-skills bullet, always appended after whatever's already there, never at the front) — with every route that reads bullets back now sorting by it. Deliberately does not retroactively fix a resume uploaded before this change: its bullets simply have no meaningful position yet, so they keep displaying in whatever order they currently do rather than having a new, still-arbitrary order silently imposed on them.

**A per-application resume title (`tailoredTitle`) is suggested from the JD's own title, minus a fixed seniority/scope word list — never generated, and never verified.** Real recruiter feedback flagged a JD's exact title as "not found in your resume," and separately, a genuine multi-role situation came up: the same underlying full-stack/AI work legitimately supports being framed as a Software Engineer, Full-Stack Engineer, Backend Engineer, or AI Engineer depending on which JD it's aimed at, but the master resume's tagline can only say one of those at a time. Considered generating a title with an LLM call — rejected, since a title is exactly the kind of claim ("you are a Senior X") that's easy to accidentally inflate, and this pipeline's whole design already avoids letting the model invent anything about seniority or scope. Considered copying the JD's title verbatim — rejected on its own, since a JD title can itself claim a seniority/scope level ("Senior", "Staff", "Lead", "Principal", "Founding", "Head of", "Chief", "VP", "Director") the resume doesn't back up. Chosen instead: a small deterministic function (`suggestResumeTitle.js`) that strips that fixed, hand-maintained word list from the JD's own title text — the same "explicit short list, not a broad pattern" precedent already used for `NON_VERIFIABLE_CLAIM_IDS` — falling back to the resume's existing title unchanged if nothing usable is left. Because it only ever removes words from real, already-known text (or falls back to the resume's own existing title), there's no fabrication surface at all, so node 6 doesn't verify it, for the same reason `tailoredSkills` doesn't either. Given the same `{generatedText, humanEditedText, finalText, editSource}` shape as `tailoredSummary`, including the identical retry-preservation behavior (this time built correctly from the start, unlike `tailoredSummary`'s own resync bug documented above) — confirmed live by hand-editing a suggested title, retrying, and seeing the edit survive. Also newly threaded into node 9's ATS/recruiter prompt as `<resume_title>`, since that node previously never saw the resume's title at all and couldn't judge title-to-role alignment as part of its score/feedback — confirmed live: a JD titled "Lead Backend Engineer" against a resume with no leadership bullets produced a suggested title of "Backend Engineer" (the honest, unsupported "Lead" dropped) and recruiter feedback that correctly asked for leadership evidence instead of a generic title-mismatch complaint.

**A hand-edited title gets one narrow, informational-only seniority-word warning — not the same fabrication check bullets/summary get, and not extended to catch domain fabrication either.** A hand-edited `tailoredTitle` bypasses the deterministic fabrication check entirely, same as a hand-edited bullet or summary (`trustHumanEdit()` always passes) — that's deliberate and unchanged, since a human edit is your own claim about yourself, not the AI's. But unlike bullets/summary, title already has a fixed, exact, hand-maintained list of words we know this resume can't back up (`SENIORITY_QUALIFIER_TERMS`, the same list the AI suggestion strips from the JD title). Considered leaving this alone, matching bullets/summary exactly — rejected, since re-checking a hand-edited title against a list we already maintain for the AI path is essentially free and catches a real, concrete risk (typing "Chief" or "Director" back into a title the AI had deliberately trimmed). Considered extending this into a general title-fabrication check (e.g. flagging a hand-edited title claiming an unsupported domain like "Machine Learning Engineer") — rejected for now as a materially bigger feature: it would require comparing the title's implied domain against canonical skills actually present in the kept bullets, not a word-list lookup, and wasn't what was asked for. Chosen: `findUnsupportedSeniorityTerms()` reuses the exact same word list, runs only against a hand-edited title (the AI-generated one can never contain one of these words by construction), and surfaces as a plain inline warning next to the title on the Approval page — never blocking the save, computed live on every `GET /:id` (not just right after the edit) so it survives a page reload, the same "compute live, never persist" pattern already used for `verifiedSkills`/`skillMatchTypes`.

**The Approval page now tracks whether the on-screen score/feedback is stale relative to your last edit, and prompts you to Re-check.** Before this, nothing distinguished "the score you're looking at reflects what's currently saved" from "you just toggled a bullet's include/exclude checkbox and the score hasn't caught up yet" — Re-check has always been a manual, on-request action, so a stale score could sit on screen indefinitely with no visual difference from a current one. Chosen: a purely client-side `dirtySinceCheck` flag, set the moment any edit/include-exclude action saves successfully (bullet text, bullet include/exclude, summary, title, or skills), and cleared either by running Re-check or by a retry ("send back with notes"), since a retry already re-runs the same human-recheck computation on your behalf as part of that flow — confirmed by tracing that `sendBackWithNotes` and `acceptSuggestedSkill` both route through the same `loadApplication()` reload this flag also resets on. Surfaced as a fixed-position blue info toast in the bottom corner of the viewport (not an inline card banner) with its own "Re-check edited text" action button, so it stays visible and actionable regardless of how far you've scrolled down the page — deliberately styled as a distinct "info" color rather than reusing the destructive/error red already used elsewhere on this page, since a stale score is a normal, expected state to pass through, not a problem. No backend change needed at all, since this is purely about the state of the page you're looking at, not a new fact about the application itself.

**A fourth node-5 deterministic guarantee reverts a bullet to its verbatim source text if the model's own rephrase dropped a JD-matched skill that was literally in the original.** Found on a real application: a bullet explicitly listing "MongoDB, Express.js, React, Node.js" got STAR-rephrased down to "the MERN stack" — true in meaning, but "MERN" isn't a registered alias for `express` specifically, so the literal word vanished from the exported text. The keyword-gap list then correctly reported `express` as missing, per its own documented job (it only ever looks at what's actually in the exported file) — that part wasn't the bug. The actual gap: node 5's only hard rule was "never invent a skill absent from the source," with nothing stopping it from removing one that was already there. Considered leaving this to the prompt (an instruction like "don't drop a mentioned skill") — rejected for the same reason force-including a skill-gap bullet and guaranteeing employer coverage were both made deterministic rather than left to model compliance: this is a concretely checkable condition, and a soft instruction isn't reliable enough for something checkable. Chosen: `preserveMatchedSkillWording()` compares canonical skills extracted from a kept bullet's source text against its tailored text (reusing `extractClaimedSkills`, already used the same way in `verifiedSkills.js`); if a skill is in both the source text and `matchedSkills` (the JD∩resume overlap) but missing from the tailored text, the bullet reverts to its verbatim source — the same safe, zero-fabrication-risk fallback the other two node-5 guarantees already use, rather than trying to surgically re-insert one word into an arbitrary sentence. Deliberately scoped to `matchedSkills` only (an irrelevant dropped word doesn't matter) and skips any bullet with `editSource: "human"` (a human's own rewording is never second-guessed), matching the same rules the other guarantees already follow.

**The tailored summary now opens with the actual `tailoredTitle`, instead of a role phrase the model infers on its own from the bullets.** Found live: the Title section could show "Senior Software Engineer" while the Summary a few lines below opened with "...as a Fullstack & AI Engineer" — two independent framings of the same resume, because summary generation never actually received the tailored title as an input at all; it only ever saw the kept bullets, matched skills, and years of experience. Chosen fix: compute `tailoredTitle` *before* calling the tailoring model (it never depended on that call's output anyway — only on `jdTitle`/`resumeTitle`/a prior human edit, all already available earlier), then pass its `finalText` in as a fourth input the summary must open with verbatim, replacing the old "role implied by the kept bullets" instruction. `computeInputHash` and `TAILOR_PROMPT_VERSION` (bumped to `tailor-v11`) both updated so a title change correctly invalidates the cache and old cached generations made without this input aren't silently reused. Because title is computed first and a hand-edited title already wins over a fresh AI suggestion, a hand-edited title now also becomes what the next retry's summary opens with — not just what the Title section displays — for free, with no separate wiring needed.

**Removed the static resume-preview tagline once the Title section could show the same thing, plus more.** The read-only "Your resume" card at the top of the Approval page used to show `masterResume.personalInfo.title` as a standalone line above the candidate's name. Once the dedicated Title section existed (Original/Tailored/Edit), that line became pure duplication — the Title section already shows the exact same "Original" value, plus the tailored one. Removed rather than kept as a fallback; an application created before `tailoredTitle` existed simply shows no title indicator until its next retry, the same "not worth a backfill for a self-healing gap" tradeoff already made for `ResumeBullet.order`.
